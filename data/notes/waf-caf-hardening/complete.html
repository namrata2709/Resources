📘 Introduction

When companies move to the cloud, two big questions arise: "Are we using AWS the right way?" and "Is our infrastructure actually secure?" This session answers both — through four interconnected frameworks and concepts that appear heavily in the AWS Cloud Practitioner exam.

⚡ What You'll Learn:
  • WAF — The AWS Well-Architected Framework: 6 pillars to evaluate your cloud architecture quality
  • CAF — The Cloud Adoption Framework: 6 perspectives to prepare your organisation for cloud migration
  • Network Hardening — Controlling access before someone reaches your system
  • System Hardening — Controlling access after someone is inside your system

Here's how these four fit together: CAF helps you plan and migrate, WAF helps you review and improve once you're in AWS, and Hardening (both types) keeps your infrastructure secure throughout. Think of it as: plan → build → review → secure.

Figure 1: How WAF, CAF, and Hardening fit into your cloud journey

💡 Exam tip: These four topics regularly appear together in Cloud Practitioner exam questions. The most common trap is confusing WAF and CAF — by the end of these notes, that distinction will be crystal clear.

🏛️ 1. AWS Well-Architected Framework (WAF)

Imagine you just moved into a new house 🏠. The construction is done, but now you need to make sure everything runs properly — electricity, plumbing, security alarms, energy efficiency. Moving in was just the first step!

That's exactly what the WAF is. The AWS Well-Architected Framework (WAF) is a set of best practices to design, build, and review workloads on AWS. It's not a service you turn on — it's a framework, a structured guide that helps you evaluate and improve your cloud setup after you've already built it.

💡 What problems does WAF solve? After companies migrate to AWS, they often face unexpected issues: huge bills at month-end, security breaches, no disaster recovery plan, poor performance under load. The WAF gives them a structured checklist to continuously review and improve their cloud architecture.

Think of WAF as a quality audit system — like how hospitals have periodic inspections to ensure they're following all best practices. WAF lets you inspect your AWS architecture against industry standards, anytime, for free.

Figure 2: The 6 Pillars of the AWS Well-Architected Framework

✅ Critical distinction: WAF is NOT a service — it is a framework for evaluating the technical correctness and quality of your AWS workloads. It is applied after migration, during ongoing operations.

According to AWS documentation, the Well-Architected Framework is built around a set of foundational questions that help cloud architects understand if their workloads align with best practices.

📚 Source: AWS Well-Architected Framework Docs

🏗️ 2. The 6 Pillars of WAF

The WAF is built on 6 pillars. Think of these as the 6 departments of a well-run company. Each focuses on a different dimension of your cloud health — and weakness in any one pillar affects the whole.

Figure 3: The 6 WAF Pillars — focus, services, and key questions

🖥️ Pillar 1: Operational Excellence

What it means: Running and monitoring systems to deliver business value, and continuously improving processes and procedures over time.

Real-world analogy: Think of a restaurant kitchen. Operational Excellence means the kitchen has clear workflows, chefs know their roles, every order is tracked, and the head chef regularly reviews what went wrong to improve next time. The kitchen doesn't just cook — it learns and gets better every shift.

In AWS terms: Automate deployments using CodePipeline and CodeDeploy, monitor with CloudWatch, implement infrastructure as code using CloudFormation, run frequent small changes rather than big risky ones, and document runbooks for common incidents.

⚡ Key question: "Are we running our systems efficiently AND improving from every incident?"
✅ Key practices: Perform operations as code, make frequent small reversible changes, anticipate failure, and learn from all operational events.

🔒 Pillar 2: Security

What it means: Protecting your information, systems, and assets while delivering business value through risk assessment and mitigation strategies.

Real-world analogy: Security is like the lock on your front door, the CCTV camera outside, and the safe inside your bedroom — multiple independent layers of protection. If one layer fails, the others still hold.

In AWS terms: Use IAM for identity and access control, enable MFA on all accounts, encrypt data at rest (KMS) and in transit (TLS), enable CloudTrail for complete audit logs, use Security Groups and NACLs for network access control, and enable GuardDuty for threat detection.

✅ Security covers: Identity management, detective controls, infrastructure protection, data protection, and incident response.
⚡ Key question: "Is every access decision deliberate — and can I prove it?"

🔄 Pillar 3: Reliability

What it means: Ensuring a workload performs its intended function correctly and consistently when it's expected to — including the ability to recover automatically from infrastructure or service disruptions.

Real-world analogy: A reliable car starts every time you turn the key — in rain, cold weather, or after sitting unused for a week. Reliability in cloud means your app keeps working even when one server crashes, one availability zone goes down, or traffic spikes unexpectedly.

In AWS terms: Use Auto Scaling so your app handles traffic spikes automatically, Multi-AZ deployments so one data centre failure doesn't take you down, Route 53 health checks for failover routing, backups with S3 and RDS snapshots, and design for distributed failures.

⚡ Key question: "Can my system recover automatically from failures — without human intervention?"

⚡ Pillar 4: Performance Efficiency

What it means: Using IT and compute resources efficiently — getting maximum performance from the resources you use, and maintaining that efficiency as demand changes and technology evolves.

Real-world analogy: A fuel-efficient car gets more kilometres per litre. Performance efficiency in cloud means you're not over-provisioning (paying for resources you don't use) or under-provisioning (suffering slow performance during peak load).

In AWS terms: Choose the right EC2 instance type and size for your workload, use serverless (Lambda) where appropriate to eliminate idle capacity, use CloudFront CDN to serve content closer to users, use ElastiCache to cache frequently accessed data, and continuously benchmark your architecture.

✅ Performance efficiency is about the right resource, at the right size, at the right time.
⚡ Key question: "Am I using the best resource for this job — and am I measuring it?"

💰 Pillar 5: Cost Optimization

What it means: Avoiding unnecessary costs by understanding and controlling where money is being spent, and delivering business value at the lowest possible price point.

Real-world analogy: Turning off lights when you leave a room. In cloud, this means shutting down unused EC2 instances during nights and weekends, deleting old EBS snapshots, right-sizing your infrastructure, and choosing the cheapest storage tier for data you rarely access.

In AWS terms: Use Reserved Instances for predictable workloads (save up to 72%), Spot Instances for flexible batch jobs (save up to 90%), AWS Cost Explorer to track and analyse spending, S3 lifecycle policies to automatically move old data to Glacier, and AWS Trusted Advisor for cost recommendations.

⚡ Key question: "Am I paying for anything I don't actually need right now?"

🌱 Pillar 6: Sustainability

What it means: Minimizing the environmental impacts of running cloud workloads by reducing energy consumption and increasing efficiency across your entire workload lifecycle.

Real-world analogy: Choosing a hybrid car over a petrol-only car to reduce your carbon footprint — getting the same transportation value with less environmental cost.

In AWS terms: Use managed services like Lambda and Fargate (less infrastructure to idle), select Regions powered by renewable energy, optimise code to run faster with less compute, use Graviton processors (better performance per watt), and shut down development environments when not in use.

✅ Sustainability was added as the 6th pillar in 2021 — the newest addition to WAF. Expect at least one question about it in the exam!
📝 Memory Trick for 6 Pillars: OSRPCS — Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, Sustainability. Remember: "Our Security Really Performs Cost Sustainably" 🎯

🗺️ 3. AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF)

Now here's a completely different scenario. Your company has decided to move to the cloud — but you haven't started yet. Leadership is asking: "Are we even READY to migrate?" That's where the CAF comes in.

The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF) helps organisations understand how to prepare for cloud migration — it is a framework for organisational readiness, not technical architecture.

💡 Key Insight: Moving to the cloud is NOT just a technology change — it's an organisational transformation. You need to train people, update processes, change how teams collaborate, adjust governance policies, and ensure security compliance. CAF addresses every one of these dimensions.

Think of it like relocating your entire company headquarters to a new city. You don't just move the computers — HR manages employee transitions, Finance budgets the relocation, IT sets up the new network, Legal reviews contracts, and Management communicates the strategy to everyone. CAF is the framework that covers all these angles for cloud migration.

Figure 4: The 6 Perspectives of the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework

According to AWS documentation, the CAF groups its guidance into six perspectives — each covering distinct responsibilities owned by different stakeholder groups in your organisation.

📚 Source: AWS Cloud Adoption Framework

🔭 4. The 6 Perspectives of CAF

CAF has 6 perspectives — not "pillars" like WAF, but "perspectives." Each perspective represents a different stakeholder group and their unique concerns about cloud migration.

💼 Perspective 1: Business

Focus: Ensuring cloud investments align with business outcomes and deliver measurable return on investment.

Who's involved: Business Managers, Finance Managers, Budget Owners, Strategy Stakeholders, C-Suite executives.

What they're asking: "How does moving to cloud align with our business goals? What's the ROI? How do we justify this investment to the board? Will this help us grow faster?"

⚡ Business perspective ensures cloud migration serves actual business outcomes — not just IT goals. If the business case isn't clear, the migration shouldn't proceed.

👥 Perspective 2: People

Focus: People development, training, communications, organisational change management, and building a cloud-ready culture.

Who's involved: Human Resources, Staffing, People Managers, Learning & Development teams.

What they're asking: "Do our employees have the skills to work in a cloud environment? How do we train them? How do we manage the cultural shift from on-premises thinking to cloud-first thinking?"

✅ This perspective is the most commonly overlooked — but technology changes fail when people aren't prepared. CAF makes human factors a first-class concern, not an afterthought.

📊 Perspective 3: Governance

Focus: Managing and measuring cloud investments, maintaining compliance, and ensuring the migration delivers expected business value.

Who's involved: CIO, Program Managers, Project Managers, Enterprise Architects, Business Analysts, Portfolio Managers.

What they're asking: "How do we track our cloud spending? How do we ensure regulatory compliance? What KPIs measure cloud migration success? Who approves major architecture decisions?"

⚡ Governance is about control and accountability — making sure the migration stays on track, within budget, and delivers the expected value to the business.

🖥️ Perspective 4: Platform

Focus: Designing, implementing, and optimising the cloud infrastructure and architecture that will host migrated and new workloads.

Who's involved: CTO, IT Managers, Solution Architects, Platform Engineers.

What they're asking: "How do we architect our applications for the cloud? Which AWS services do we use? How do we design our VPCs, networking, and data layers? What does our landing zone look like?"

✅ Platform perspective is the most technical of the six — it's about the actual cloud infrastructure design, architecture patterns, and implementation decisions.

⚙️ Perspective 5: Operations

Focus: Defining how cloud workloads will be monitored, managed, and supported on an ongoing basis after migration is complete.

Who's involved: IT Operations Managers, IT Support Managers, Site Reliability Engineers.

What they're asking: "How do we monitor our cloud systems? How do we handle incidents at 2am? How do we maintain uptime and SLAs in the cloud? Who's on call?"

⚡ Operations perspective ensures day-to-day cloud management is smooth and reliable — it's the "what happens after we go live" perspective.

🔐 Perspective 6: Security

Focus: Ensuring that workloads deployed in the cloud meet the organisation's security controls, resiliency requirements, and regulatory compliance obligations.

Who's involved: CISO, IT Security Managers, IT Security Analysts, Head of Audit and Compliance, Data Protection Officers.

What they're asking: "Are our cloud workloads secure? Do we meet compliance requirements like GDPR or HIPAA? How do we handle data protection and encryption? How do we detect and respond to threats?"

✅ Security in CAF is about organisational compliance and policy — making sure the migration follows security frameworks, regulations, and internal policies. This is different from WAF's Security pillar which is about technical implementation.
📝 Memory Trick for 6 Perspectives: BGPPOS — Business, Governance, People, Platform, Operations, Security. Or: "Big Governments Prefer Proper Operational Security" 🎯

🔐 6. Why Hardening is Needed

Here's a fundamental truth of cloud security: When you install an operating system or provision a server, it is configured for ease of use by default — not for security. Hardening is the process of deliberately securing it by removing unnecessary features and controlling access.

Let's use an analogy. Imagine you have a gold bar 🥇 worth ₹50 lakhs. You don't just leave it on your desk! You put it in a locker → inside a cupboard → inside a locked bedroom → inside a secured house with a gate → with CCTV cameras watching who enters. Multiple layers of protection, both before someone reaches the house and after they're inside.

In the digital world, servers and data need the same layered protection:

  • Before someone reaches the system → This is Network Hardening
  • After someone is inside the system → This is System Hardening
💡 Why is hardening critical in AWS? AWS uses a Shared Responsibility Model. AWS secures the infrastructure; you are responsible for securing your operating systems, applications, data, and access controls. Hardening is how you fulfil your side of that responsibility.

🌐 7. Network Hardening

Network hardening is the process of controlling access to a system at the network level — before traffic even reaches the server. It is your first line of defence.

Think of it as the security at the entrance of your apartment building — the guard at the gate, the locked main door, the intercom system. If an unauthorised person can't get through the gate, they can never reach your front door, let alone break in.

Network hardening answers three essential questions:

  • 🔸 Who is allowed to connect? (Which IP addresses or IP ranges?)
  • 🔸 From where can they connect? (Which networks or locations?)
  • 🔸 On which port can they connect? (Which protocol and service?)

Figure 6: Network Hardening Architecture — Firewall, IDPS, Core Infrastructure, and VPN Gateway

🔧 Components of Network Hardening

🛡️ Firewall

A firewall is the primary gatekeeper of your network. It examines every packet of incoming and outgoing traffic and decides whether to allow or block it based on pre-defined rules.

  • ACLs (Access Control Lists): Rules that define exactly what traffic is allowed or denied — by source IP, destination IP, port, and protocol
  • Port blocking: Block specific ports (e.g., block port 23 Telnet which sends credentials in plaintext, allow only port 443 HTTPS)
  • Geo-blocking: Block traffic from specific countries or IP ranges where your users never operate
  • Stateful inspection: Tracks the state of connections so only legitimate responses to outbound requests are allowed back in

AWS equivalents: Security Groups (instance-level, stateful) and Network ACLs (subnet-level, stateless) act as firewalls in AWS. AWS WAF protects web applications from HTTP-level attacks.

🚨 IDPS — Intrusion Detection and Prevention System

If the firewall is the locked gate, IDPS is the security camera and alarm system that watches for suspicious behaviour — even from users who got through the gate legitimately.

  • Real-time alerts: Immediately notifies administrators of suspicious activity patterns
  • Anomaly detection: Flags unusual patterns — for example, a user downloading 10GB of data at 3am when they normally download 100MB during business hours
  • Prevention mode: Automatically blocks detected threats, not just alerts on them

AWS equivalent: Amazon GuardDuty provides intelligent threat detection for your entire AWS environment — analysing CloudTrail logs, VPC Flow Logs, and DNS logs to detect malicious activity.

🏗️ Core Infrastructure Hardening

The physical and logical network devices that route traffic through your organisation also need hardening — attackers target routers and switches too.

  • Router: Directs traffic between different networks — must have default passwords changed and access lists configured
  • Switch: Connects devices within the same network — disable unused ports, enable port security
  • Wireless access point: Provides WiFi — must use WPA3 encryption, disable WPS, use separate SSIDs for guests vs internal users

Key hardening actions: Change all default passwords immediately, apply firmware updates, disable unused management interfaces, enable logging on all devices.

🔒 VPN Gateway

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) creates an encrypted tunnel for remote access — like a secure underground passage into your building that no one on the street can see or intercept.

  • IPsec tunnels: Encrypts all data flowing between the remote user and your network, making interception useless
  • MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication): Requires two or more forms of identity verification before the VPN connects — even if a password is stolen, the attacker still can't connect

AWS equivalents: AWS Site-to-Site VPN for connecting your office network to AWS, and AWS Client VPN for individual remote users to securely access AWS resources.

✅ Practical AWS Example of Network Hardening:
In your EC2 Security Group, you configure:
• Port 80 — open from the internet (0.0.0.0/0) for web traffic
• Port 22 (SSH) — open ONLY from your admin IP address (e.g., 203.0.113.5/32)
• All other ports — blocked by default (Security Groups deny all by default)

This means even if an attacker discovers your server's public IP address, they cannot SSH into it unless they're physically on your admin machine. That's network hardening in action!

🖥️ 8. System Hardening

System hardening is the process of securing a computer system by reducing its attack surface — controlling what users can do, which software runs, and how files are accessed once someone is inside the system.

Continuing the apartment analogy: you've passed the building gate (network hardening). Now you're inside the building lobby. System hardening is about what you can do inside — which floors you can access with your key card, which rooms you can enter, which files in the office you can read or modify.

Figure 7: The 5 Layers of System Hardening — each layer protects the one inside it

🔧 What System Hardening Controls

💻 Operating System (OS) Hardening

The OS is the foundation of everything — securing it protects all software and data running on top of it.

  • Remove unnecessary software and services that come installed by default — every running service is a potential attack entry point
  • Apply all security patches and updates promptly — most successful attacks exploit known vulnerabilities that have patches available
  • Configure strong password policies (minimum length, complexity, expiry)
  • Disable unnecessary user accounts that are created by default during OS installation
  • Enable audit logging to track who did what and when
  • Disable root login via SSH — use a normal account with sudo instead

Example: A fresh Amazon Linux 2 EC2 instance has multiple services running by default. OS hardening disables FTP, Telnet, unused cron jobs, and other unnecessary services — keeping only what the server actually needs for its role.

AWS tool: AWS Systems Manager Patch Manager automates OS patching across all your EC2 instances on a schedule.

👤 User Access Control

Who gets access to what inside the system? This is the digital equivalent of the key card access system in an office building.

  • Principle of Least Privilege — give users only the minimum permissions they need to perform their specific job, and nothing more.
  • Separate admin accounts from regular user accounts — never use root for everyday tasks
  • Use role-based access control (RBAC) — access is tied to job function, not individual
  • Regularly review and revoke access for users who changed roles or left the organisation
  • Implement strong multi-factor authentication for all privileged accounts

AWS equivalent: AWS IAM (Identity and Access Management) implements least privilege through policies, roles, and permission boundaries. IAM Roles are preferred over IAM Users for applications.

⚡ Key principle: Users should have the minimum permissions needed to do their job — no more, no less. This limits the damage if an account is compromised.

🔧 Service Hardening

Which services and processes are allowed to run on the system? Every running service increases the attack surface.

  • Disable services not required for the system's specific purpose — a web server doesn't need a mail server or FTP service
  • Use service accounts with minimal permissions — never run a web server as root
  • Regularly audit all running services: systemctl list-units --type=service --state=running
  • Set services to not start automatically on boot if they're not needed

Example: A dedicated web server should only run Apache/Nginx and nothing else. All other services (postfix, vsftpd, bluetooth, etc.) should be disabled and stopped.

📁 File and Permission Management

Who can read, write, or execute which files on the system? Misconfigured file permissions are one of the most common security vulnerabilities.

  • Set proper file permissions using the principle of least privilege — sensitive config files readable only by root or the specific service account
  • Encrypt sensitive files and directories at rest using tools like LUKS (Linux) or BitLocker (Windows)
  • Regular permission audits to find world-readable sensitive files: find / -perm -o+r -type f 2>/dev/null
  • File integrity monitoring — detect and alert when critical system files are unexpectedly changed

AWS equivalent: AWS KMS (Key Management Service) encrypts data at rest across S3, RDS, EBS, and other services. AWS CloudTrail tracks all API calls for auditing.

🗄️ Database Hardening

Databases store your most valuable data — customer information, financial records, personal data. They need extra layers of protection on top of OS and network controls.

  • Remove default database users (like MySQL's anonymous user) and sample databases that come with fresh installations
  • Encrypt data at rest and in transit — all data flowing between app servers and databases must be encrypted
  • Restrict database access to only the specific application servers that need it — no direct developer access to production databases
  • Implement query monitoring and logging to detect unusual access patterns (e.g., SELECT * on customer table from a new IP)
  • Use strong, unique passwords for all database accounts and rotate them regularly

AWS equivalents: Amazon RDS with encryption enabled, IAM database authentication (no passwords needed — uses AWS credentials), Security Group rules restricting DB access to the application tier only, and automated backups.

🖥️ Server Hardening

Physical and virtual server-level security that wraps around all other hardening layers.

  • Keep OS and all software updated with the latest security patches — unpatched servers are the #1 cause of breaches
  • Remove unnecessary software (reduce bloat — fewer packages means fewer vulnerabilities)
  • Configure host-based firewalls (iptables or firewalld on Linux) as a secondary layer inside network-level firewalls
  • Enable host-based intrusion detection (AIDE, Tripwire) to detect unexpected file changes or rootkits
  • Disable remote management protocols not in use (Telnet, RDP if not needed)

AWS tool: AWS Systems Manager provides patch management, configuration compliance, and remote management without opening SSH ports — a best practice for production environments.

❌ Critical Mistake: People think setting up a server securely once is enough. Hardening is not a one-time activity — new vulnerabilities (CVEs) are discovered regularly, and systems must be continuously monitored, patched, and audited.

📝 Overall Summary

  • WAF (Well-Architected Framework): A set of best practices for the technical quality of AWS workloads — applied AFTER migration. Has 6 pillars: Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, Sustainability.
  • WAF is NOT a service: It's a framework (a structured guide) to evaluate and improve your cloud architecture quality at any time.
  • The 6th pillar Sustainability was added in 2021 — the newest addition to WAF. All other pillars were present from the beginning.
  • CAF (Cloud Adoption Framework): Helps organisations prepare for cloud migration — focuses on organisational readiness. Has 6 perspectives: Business, People, Governance, Platform, Operations, Security.
  • WAF vs CAF: WAF = technical correctness AFTER migration; CAF = organisational readiness BEFORE/DURING migration. WAF has Pillars; CAF has Perspectives.
  • Cloud migration is an organisational change: It requires people training (People perspective), process updates (Governance), business alignment (Business), and not just technology changes.
  • Why hardening: Default OS and server configurations are optimised for ease of use, not security. Hardening deliberately reduces the attack surface.
  • Network Hardening: Controls access BEFORE reaching the system. Controls who connects, from where, and on which port. AWS tools: Security Groups, NACLs, GuardDuty, VPN.
  • System Hardening: Controls access AFTER reaching the system. Covers OS patching, user access, running services, file permissions, and databases. AWS tools: IAM, Systems Manager, KMS.
  • Principle of Least Privilege: A core concept in system hardening — give users and services the minimum permissions needed, nothing more.
  • Hardening is ongoing: New CVEs are discovered constantly. Hardening is a continuous process of monitoring, patching, and auditing — not a one-time task.

🔴 Project 1: Break Your Own Security — Then Fix It

🎯 Objective: Intentionally misconfigure a Security Group, observe the exposure using a port scanner, then harden it. This flips your thinking from setup → risk analysis.

⏱️ Time: 30-40 min | 💰 Cost: Free Tier

Phase 1 — The Broken Setup

  1. Go to EC2 → Launch Instance → Amazon Linux 2, t2.micro
  2. Create a new Security Group: broken-sg
  3. Add these INTENTIONALLY WRONG inbound rules:
    • SSH (port 22) → Source: 0.0.0.0/0 ← open to the entire internet ❌
    • HTTP (port 80) → Source: 0.0.0.0/0
    • All traffic → Source: 0.0.0.0/0 ← worst possible configuration ❌
  4. Launch the instance and note its Public IP
  5. 📸 Screenshot: The Security Group inbound rules showing all ports open to 0.0.0.0/0

    6. 📸 Screenshot 1: Broken Security Group — All ports open to 0.0.0.0/0

Phase 2 — Observe the Exposure

  1. Go to pentest-tools.com (free online port scanner)
  2. Enter your EC2 instance's Public IP and run a scan
  3. Observe: SSH port 22 is publicly visible — any attacker in the world can attempt to brute-force it
  4. 📸 Screenshot: Port scanner results showing SSH port 22 publicly exposed

    5. 📸 Screenshot 2: Port scanner confirming SSH port 22 is publicly visible to the internet

Phase 3 — Fix It (Network Hardening)

  1. Go to EC2 → Security Groups → Select broken-sg → Edit inbound rules
  2. DELETE the "All traffic" rule entirely
  3. CHANGE the SSH rule: Source → My IP (AWS automatically fills your current IP)
  4. KEEP HTTP (port 80) with Source: 0.0.0.0/0 (web traffic must remain public)
  5. Save rules
  6. 📸 Screenshot: The corrected, hardened Security Group inbound rules

    7. 📸 Screenshot 3: Hardened Security Group — SSH to My IP only, HTTP public

  7. Re-run the port scanner — SSH port 22 should no longer appear publicly
  8. Try SSH from your phone's mobile hotspot — it should fail with a timeout ✅
  9. Cleanup: Terminate the EC2 instance
✅ Expected result: Port 22 disappears from public scanner. SSH works only from your admin IP. HTTP still accessible from everywhere.
🔍 Think Like an Engineer — answer these:
  • Why is port 22 open to 0.0.0.0/0 dangerous even if you have a 20-character password?
  • Why must port 80 stay public while port 22 should be restricted?
  • What happens if your home IP changes — how would you update the SSH rule without losing access?
  • WAF Pillar: Which pillar did we just improve? (Security ✅)

👤 Project 2: IAM Mistake Lab — Feel Least Privilege

🎯 Objective: Create an over-privileged IAM user, experience what unrestricted access feels like, then apply Least Privilege. The moment you try to do something you shouldn't and get blocked — that's when the concept truly clicks.

⏱️ Time: 25-35 min | 💰 Cost: Free

Phase 1 — The Over-Privileged User (Wrong Way)

  1. IAM → Users → Add users → Name: test-admin-user
  2. Select: Provide user access to AWS Management Console
  3. Permissions: Attach policies directly → Add AdministratorAccess ❌
  4. 📸 Screenshot: AdministratorAccess policy attached to the test user

    5. 📸 Screenshot 4: IAM user with AdministratorAccess — over-privileged ❌

  5. Create the user and note the console sign-in URL and credentials
  6. Open a new incognito/private browser window
  7. Sign in as test-admin-user
  8. Observe: This user can delete S3 buckets, launch EC2 instances, view billing — everything ⚠️

Phase 2 — Apply Least Privilege (Right Way)

  1. Back in your main admin browser → IAM → Users → test-admin-user

🏰 Project 4: Secure VPC Architecture — The Interview Gold Project

🎯 Objective: Build a proper VPC with public and private subnets. Web server in public subnet, database in private subnet. SSH only via Bastion Host. This single project covers network hardening, system design, and security architecture in one practical build.

⏱️ Time: 75-90 min | 💰 Cost: Free Tier (avoid NAT Gateway — ~$0.05/hr)

Architecture to Build

📐 Target Architecture:

Internet
    ↓
Internet Gateway
    ↓
[PUBLIC SUBNET 10.0.1.0/24]
    ├── Bastion Host (SSH access point — My IP only)
    └── Web Server (HTTP/HTTPS public)
    ↓ (only via Security Group rules — no public route)
[PRIVATE SUBNET 10.0.2.0/24]
    └── DB Server (no public IP, no direct internet access)

Step 1 — Create the VPC

  1. VPC → Your VPCs → Create VPC
  2. Name: secure-vpc, IPv4 CIDR: 10.0.0.0/16
  3. 📸 Screenshot: VPC created with CIDR block in Available state

    4. 📸 Screenshot 11: secure-vpc created with CIDR 10.0.0.0/16

Step 2 — Create Subnets

  1. VPC → Subnets → Create subnet → VPC: secure-vpc
  2. Subnet 1: Name: public-subnet, AZ: your region's first AZ, CIDR: 10.0.1.0/24
  3. Subnet 2: Name: private-subnet, same AZ, CIDR: 10.0.2.0/24
  4. For public-subnet only: Actions → Edit subnet settings → Enable auto-assign public IPv4 address
  5. 📸 Screenshot: Both subnets created, showing public vs private distinction

    6. 📸 Screenshot 12: Public subnet with auto-assign IP enabled; private subnet without

Step 3 — Internet Gateway

  1. VPC → Internet Gateways → Create → Name: secure-igw
  2. Actions → Attach to VPC → select secure-vpc
  3. VPC → Route Tables → Find the route table for public-subnet
  4. Edit routes → Add: Destination 0.0.0.0/0 → Target: secure-igw
  5. 📸 Screenshot: Public subnet route table with internet gateway route added

    6. 📸 Screenshot 13: Route table with 0.0.0.0/0 → Internet Gateway for public subnet only

Step 4 — Create Security Groups

  1. Bastion SG (bastion-sg): SSH port 22 → Source: My IP only
  2. Web SG (web-sg): HTTP port 80 → 0.0.0.0/0 | SSH port 22 → Source: bastion-sg
  3. DB SG (db-sg): MySQL port 3306 → Source: web-sg only | SSH port 22 → Source: bastion-sg only
  4. 📸 Screenshot: All 3 Security Groups showing their inbound rules

    5. 📸 Screenshot 14: All 3 Security Groups with rules referencing each other

Step 5 — Launch Instances

  1. Bastion Host: t2.micro → public-subnet → bastion-sg → Name: bastion-host
  2. Web Server: t2.micro → public-subnet → web-sg → Name: web-server
  3. DB Server: t2.micro → private-subnet → db-sg → No public IP → Name: db-server
  4. 📸 Screenshot: EC2 console showing all 3 instances — note DB has no Public IP at all

    5. 📸 Screenshot 15: DB instance has no Public IP — completely unreachable from internet ✅

Step 6 — Verify the Architecture

  1. SSH to Bastion Host directly from your machine ✅
  2. From Bastion, SSH to DB Server using its Private IP ✅
  3. Try SSH directly to DB Server from your machine → Should fail (no public IP, no route) ✅
  4. 📸 Screenshot: SSH chain — your machine → Bastion → DB Server

    5. 📸 Screenshot 16: SSH hop chain — local → bastion → DB server via private IP

  5. Cleanup: Terminate all 3 instances → Delete Security Groups → Detach and Delete IGW → Delete Subnets → Delete VPC
✅ What you built: A production-grade 3-tier network architecture. The DB server is completely unreachable from the internet. Even SSH to the DB goes through a single controlled entry point — the Bastion Host.

Network Hardening:

  3. 📸 Screenshot: Hardened Security Group showing minimal rules only

System Hardening (SSH into the instance):

  1. Update all packages: sudo yum update -y

📋 Project 6: CAF Simulation — Think Senior

🎯 Objective: This is a writing and thinking project — no AWS console needed. Simulate a company's cloud migration plan using all 6 CAF perspectives. Walking through this in an interview makes you look senior — most candidates completely ignore the CAF.

⏱️ Time: 45-60 min | 💰 Cost: Free (no AWS console needed)

⚡ Scenario: You are a Cloud Consultant. A mid-sized e-commerce company called "ShopEasy" runs everything on-premises in their own data centre. They want to migrate to AWS. Write their migration readiness plan covering all 6 CAF perspectives.

Your Task — Write a CAF Migration Plan

Answer each perspective for ShopEasy with 3-5 bullet points:

1. Business Perspective — Why move? What's the business case?

Example starters: Eliminate hardware refresh costs (every 3-5 years)... Scale automatically during Diwali/festive season peaks... Pay-per-use model improves cash flow versus large upfront CapEx... Faster time to market for new features... Avoid building a second data centre for disaster recovery...

2. People Perspective — Who needs training? How do you manage the cultural shift?

Example starters: 5 sysadmins need AWS training (AWS Restart or re/Start program)... Developers need to unlearn "server thinking" and learn cloud-native services... HR needs to update job descriptions to include cloud skills... Create one internal AWS Champion per team to spread knowledge...

3. Governance Perspective — How do you track and control the migration?

Example starters: Monthly AWS Cost Explorer review with budget alerts at 80% of monthly limit... Track migration progress via milestone checkpoints... Compliance check for PCI-DSS (ShopEasy processes payment card data)... Define rollback plan for each migrated workload before it goes live...

4. Platform Perspective — How do you design the cloud architecture?

Example starters: VPC with public/private subnets... EC2 for web servers, RDS for database... CloudFront CDN for static assets and faster load times globally... Auto Scaling Groups for seasonal traffic peaks... Multi-AZ RDS for database high availability...

5. Operations Perspective — How do you run it day-to-day after migration?

Example starters: CloudWatch dashboards monitoring all services... Alarms for CPU, error rate, and latency breaches... Runbooks documenting response procedures for common incidents... On-call rotation using PagerDuty or OpsGenie... Systems Manager for automated OS patching...

6. Security Perspective — How do you keep it secure and compliant?

Example starters: IAM with least privilege for all roles and service accounts... GuardDuty enabled for continuous threat detection... CloudTrail enabled for complete audit logging... KMS encryption for all customer data at rest... Quarterly access review to remove stale permissions... AWS WAF protecting the web application...

Bonus — WAF Review After Migration

Once ShopEasy is live on AWS, which WAF pillar would you review first and why? Write one paragraph justifying your choice.

✅ How to use this in interviews: "Tell me about a cloud migration project you've worked on" → Use this CAF plan as your answer structure. As a fresher, walking through all 6 perspectives shows architectural thinking that most experienced candidates skip entirely.

🤔 Reflection Questions

1. Your company just finished migrating 50 applications to AWS. A colleague says "We need to apply the CAF now." Is your colleague correct? Why or why not?

💡 Hint

Think carefully about when CAF is used versus when WAF is used. The migration is already complete — which framework applies to the state you're in now?

2. You open port 22 (SSH) on a Security Group to allow access from 0.0.0.0/0. Which hardening principle does this violate, and what is the correct fix?

💡 Hint

Network hardening principle: control WHO can connect and FROM WHERE. SSH should only be accessible from specific trusted IP addresses — not the entire internet.

3. A developer has full root access to the production database "just in case they need it someday." What hardening principle does this violate, and what risk does it create?

💡 Hint

System hardening and IAM concept: what's the minimum access someone needs to do their actual job? Think about what could happen if that developer's account is phished or their laptop is stolen.

4. Which WAF pillar is most relevant when your application crashes under unexpectedly high traffic during a festival sale? What AWS services would address this?

💡 Hint

The app was expected to work but didn't — think about which pillar ensures systems work correctly and recover from disruptions. Then think about which AWS services automatically handle traffic spikes.

5. Your company's monthly AWS bill jumped 300% unexpectedly. Which WAF pillar should you focus on first, and which specific AWS tool would you use to investigate?

💡 Hint

One pillar specifically addresses unexpected costs. AWS has a dedicated tool that provides visibility into where every dollar of cloud spend is going.

6. A new HR manager joins your company and wants to understand why they're involved in a cloud migration project. How would you explain the People perspective of CAF to them?

💡 Hint

Think about what happens when technology changes dramatically but the people using it aren't trained or supported. How does that affect the success of the migration? Use a real-world analogy.

7. Explain the difference between Network Hardening and System Hardening using the apartment building analogy. Give one AWS service example for each.

💡 Hint

Network hardening = getting through the building entrance. System hardening = what you can do once you're inside the building. Which AWS services control each layer?
