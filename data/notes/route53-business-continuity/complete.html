🌐 Introduction

Imagine you run an online store. At 2 AM your database server crashes. Two questions instantly matter: How much data did you just lose? and How long until customers can shop again? Those two questions are exactly what Business Continuity planning is about — and Amazon Route 53 is the AWS service that helps you keep traffic flowing even when things go wrong.

In this session we covered two closely related topics. First, the core language of disaster recovery — RPO (Recovery Point Objective) and RTO (Recovery Time Objective). Second, Amazon Route 53, AWS's DNS and traffic routing service, including how DNS resolution works step by step, all seven routing policies, weighted routing for deployments, and the difference between Blue/Green and Canary release strategies.

Figure 1: Session Overview — Business Continuity meets Route 53

⚡ Why this matters for the exam: RPO/RTO, Route 53 routing policies, and deployment strategies are all high-frequency exam topics. Understanding when to choose which routing policy is a classic scenario-based question.

🔥 Business Continuity — RPO & RTO

Business continuity is your organisation's plan for staying alive when disaster strikes — a server fire, a region outage, a corrupted database, or a botched deployment. Before you can design a resilient system, you need to answer two fundamental questions that define your tolerance for pain:

  1. How much data can you afford to lose? → This defines your RPO.
  2. How long can your system be down? → This defines your RTO.

Figure 2: RPO and RTO on a disaster timeline

📌 RPO — Recovery Point Objective

RPO is the maximum amount of data loss your business can tolerate, measured in time. In other words, it answers: "If disaster strikes right now, how old can my most recent backup be?"

Think of it like a save point in a video game. If you last saved 30 minutes ago and your game crashes, you lose 30 minutes of progress. Your RPO was 30 minutes. If that's acceptable to you, great. If not, you need to save more frequently.

In AWS terms: RPO is typically controlled by your backup or snapshot frequency. A database that snapshots every hour has an RPO of up to 1 hour — in the worst case, you could lose 59 minutes and 59 seconds of data. A database using continuous replication (like RDS Multi-AZ) can have an RPO of near zero, losing only seconds of data at most.

💡 Practical Example: Your system breaks at 10:00 AM. Your last backup was taken at 9:55 AM. The data between 9:55 AM and 10:00 AM is lost. Your RPO = 5 minutes.

Figure 3: Worked example — RPO 5 minutes, RTO 10 minutes

⏱️ RTO — Recovery Time Objective

RTO is the maximum acceptable amount of time for a system to be down after a disaster before it must be restored and running again. It answers: "How long can we be offline before it costs us too much?"

Back to the video game analogy: your game crashed. RPO was about how much progress you lost. RTO is about how long it takes you to restart, reload your save, and get back to playing. If you can't stand being away from the game for more than 10 minutes, your RTO is 10 minutes.

In AWS terms: RTO is controlled by how fast your recovery infrastructure can spin up and take traffic. A warm standby with pre-provisioned resources can recover in minutes. A cold backup restore from S3 might take hours.

💡 Practical Example: Continuing from above — your system broke at 10:00 AM. Your RTO = 10 minutes, so your system must be recovered and running again by 10:10 AM.

🛠️ AWS Services by RPO/RTO Level

Figure 5: AWS services grouped by RPO/RTO capability

🔵 Near-Zero RPO & RTO (Seconds)

  • RDS Multi-AZ: Synchronous standby replica in another AZ — automatic failover in 60–120 seconds.
  • Aurora Global DB: Replication to a secondary region with RPO typically under 1 second.
  • DynamoDB Global Tables: Multi-region, multi-active replication — near-zero RPO globally.
  • S3 Cross-Region Replication (CRR): Objects replicated to another region automatically.
  • Route 53 Health Checks: Automatic DNS failover when a resource becomes unhealthy.
  • Auto Scaling: Replaces failed instances automatically, reducing downtime.

🟡 Moderate RPO/RTO (Minutes to Hours)

  • RDS Snapshots: Daily automated snapshots — RPO up to 24 hours unless supplemented with transaction logs.
  • EBS Snapshots: Point-in-time snapshots stored in S3 — restore takes time proportional to volume size.
  • S3 Versioning + Lifecycle: Protects against accidental deletion; recovery is manual but fast for small datasets.

✅ Best Practice: Design your RPO/RTO targets before choosing services. Work backwards from the business requirement — not from the technology.

🛣️ What is Amazon Route 53?

Every time you type www.amazon.com into your browser, something magical happens behind the scenes: a system translates that human-readable name into a machine-readable IP address like 205.251.242.103. That system is called DNS — Domain Name System. Think of it as the phone book of the internet.

Amazon Route 53 is AWS's highly available, scalable, and fully managed DNS and traffic routing service. The name "Route 53" is a reference to port 53, the standard network port that DNS uses.

Figure 6: Route 53's four core roles

Route 53 does four things:

  1. Domain Registration: You can register domain names (like myapp.com) directly through AWS.
  2. DNS Resolution: Translates domain names to IP addresses for users worldwide.
  3. Health Checking: Monitors your endpoints and automatically routes away from unhealthy ones.
  4. Traffic Routing: Applies intelligent routing rules (latency, weight, location) to direct traffic where it should go.

📚 According to AWS documentation, Route 53 is designed to provide 100% availability SLA — it is one of the few AWS services with this guarantee.

🔄 How DNS Resolution Works (Step by Step)

Understanding how a browser turns www.site.com into an IP address is one of the most fundamental networking concepts in cloud. Here's the exact 9-step journey — the same flow shown in the session diagram:

Figure 7: Complete 9-step DNS resolution flow with Route 53 and CloudFront

  1. User types www.site.com in their browser.
  2. Query goes to DNS Resolver — usually provided by your ISP or configured on your device. The resolver is the middleman that does the heavy lifting.
  3. Resolver asks the DNS Root Name Server: "Who handles .com domains?" The root server doesn't know www.site.com specifically — it only knows which servers handle .com.
  4. Root server responds: "Go to the .com TLD (Top-Level Domain) name server."
  5. Resolver asks the TLD server: "Who is authoritative for site.com?" The TLD server knows which name server AWS Route 53 is using.
  6. TLD responds: "Go to Route 53 name servers."
  7. Resolver asks Route 53: "What is the IP for www.site.com?"
  8. Route 53 responds with the CloudFront CDN IP address (or EC2/ALB IP, depending on configuration).
  9. Resolver returns the IP to the user's browser, which then connects directly to the CDN/server and retrieves the web content.

⚡ Key insight:The DNS resolver caches responses for a period defined by the record's TTL (Time to Live). During TTL, users don't need to repeat steps 2–7 — they get the cached IP. This is why DNS changes can take time to propagate worldwide.

💡 Hosted Zones:In Route 53, a hosted zone is a container for DNS records for a specific domain. A public hosted zone routes internet traffic; a private hosted zone routes traffic only within one or more VPCs.

🗺️ Route 53 Routing Policies

A routing policy determines how Route 53 responds to DNS queries. Think of it as the intelligence layer — instead of always giving the same IP address, Route 53 can be smart about which server to point users toward based on location, health, weight, or latency. There are seven routing policies in Route 53.

Figure 8: All seven Route 53 routing policies at a glance

1️⃣ Simple Routing Policy

Simple routing sends all traffic to a single resource, and all clients receive the same response. It is the default policy — use it when you have one server and want everyone to reach it.

Figure 9: Simple Routing — one destination, all traffic

  • ✅ Use when: you have a single resource (one EC2, one ALB).
  • ✅ Can return multiple IP values — client picks one at random.
  • ❌ Simple routing does not support health checks.
  • Implementation: choose a public or private hosted zone, create a DNS record (A, AAAA, CNAME, etc.), and configure the target. No health check configuration available.

❌ Common Mistake: Students often assume Simple Routing has health check support. It does not — if your single resource fails, Route 53 will still return its IP address. Use Failover routing instead if you need health-check-aware routing.

2️⃣ Weighted Routing Policy

Weighted routing lets you assign a relative weight to each record, and Route 53 distributes traffic proportionally. If you have two records with weights 70 and 30, roughly 70% of queries go to the first resource and 30% to the second.

Figure 10: Weighted Routing — 90/10 split between old and new environments

  • ✅ Use for: A/B testing, canary deployments, gradual traffic migration between environments.
  • Weights are relative — values 0 to 255. A weight of 0 stops traffic to that record entirely.
  • Weighted routing supports health checks — unhealthy resources are automatically excluded from the response.
  • Example: During a new version rollout, send 10% traffic to the new environment and 90% to the old one. Gradually increase the new environment's weight as confidence grows.

3️⃣ Geolocation Routing Policy

Geolocation routing routes traffic based on the geographic location of the user making the DNS query. You can create records for specific continents, countries, or US states.

  • ✅ Use for: serving localised content, GDPR compliance (keeping EU data in EU), language-specific pages.
  • You can specify a default record for users whose location doesn't match any specific rule.
  • Geolocation routing is based on where the user is — not where the server with the lowest latency is. Don't confuse with Latency routing.

⚡ Exam Trap: A question asks about routing users in Germany to EU servers. The answer is Geolocation (location-based) — NOT Latency (performance-based). These are different policies with different triggers.

4️⃣ Latency Routing Policy

Latency routing directs users to the AWS Region that provides the lowest network latency for that user at that moment. It doesn't care where the user is located — it cares about the fastest response time.

  • ✅ Use for: globally distributed applications where you want the best performance for every user.
  • Works across multiple AWS Regions — Route 53 measures latency from the user to each region and picks the fastest.
  • A user in Mumbai might normally be routed to ap-south-1, but if ap-south-1 is congested, they could be routed to a less-congested region with better current latency.
  • Supports health checks — unhealthy regions are skipped.

5️⃣ Failover Routing Policy

Failover routing routes traffic to a primary resource when it is healthy, and automatically switches to a secondary resource when the primary is unhealthy.

  • ✅ Use for: active-passive disaster recovery setups.
  • Requires health checks on the primary record — this is what triggers the failover.
  • The secondary can be another region, another instance, or even a static S3 website (common exam answer for "show a maintenance page when primary is down").

💡 Classic Exam Scenario: "Your primary EC2 in us-east-1 fails. You want traffic to automatically go to eu-west-1. Which routing policy?" → Failover.

6️⃣ Multivalue Answer Routing Policy

Multivalue Answer routing returns up to 8 healthy IP addresses randomly for a single DNS query, acting like a lightweight DNS-level load balancer.

Figure 11: Multivalue Answer Routing — DNS-level load balancing with health checks

  • Each record has its own health check — unhealthy IPs are automatically removed from responses.
  • The client (browser/OS) randomly selects one of the returned IPs to connect to.
  • Multivalue Answer is not a substitute for a real load balancer — it provides simple DNS-level distribution only, with no session affinity, no SSL termination, and no path-based routing.
  • ✅ Use when: you want simple load distribution across multiple servers without advanced routing logic.

⚡ From the session — exam Q decoded: "You have two back-end servers. You want DNS to return IPs randomly, only healthy servers should be returned, simple load distribution, no advanced routing logic." → Answer: Multivalue Answer. Not Failover (that's active-passive, not random distribution). Not Latency (that's performance-based). Multivalue is the lightweight, DNS-level load distributor.

7️⃣ IP-Based Routing Policy

IP-based routing lets you route traffic based on the IP address range (CIDR block) of the client making the DNS query.

  • ✅ Use when: you know the IP ranges of your users and want to direct specific corporate networks or ISPs to specific endpoints.
  • Example: Route all traffic from your corporate VPN's IP range (e.g., 203.0.113.0/24) to an internal application server, while everyone else goes to the public server.
  • More deterministic than geolocation — you're routing by exact IP ranges, not inferred user location.

🚀 Weighted Routing for Deployments

One of the most powerful uses of Route 53 Weighted Routing is for safe application deployments. Instead of switching all users to a new version at once (risky!), you can use weighted routing to gradually shift traffic from the old environment to the new one — and roll back instantly if something goes wrong.

Figure 13: Gradual traffic shifting strategy using weighted routing

The session diagram showed this exactly: a Route 53 weighted record set splits traffic 90% to the existing production environment and 10% to the new production environment. As confidence in the new version grows, the weights shift — 80/20, then 50/50, then 0/100. Auto Scaling in the new environment adds EC2 instances as the traffic increases to handle the growing load.

🔵🟢 Blue/Green vs Canary Deployments

Two of the most important deployment strategies in modern cloud architecture — and a common exam topic — are Blue/Green and Canary deployments. Both minimize risk when releasing new software, but they do it differently.

Figure 14: Blue/Green (instant switch) vs Canary (gradual shift)

🔵🟢 Blue/Green Deployment

In a Blue/Green deployment, you maintain two complete, identical environments: the Blue environment (current production) and the Green environment (new version). When you're ready to release, you switch 100% of traffic from Blue to Green in one instant cut.

  • Blue/Green uses instant 100% traffic switching — all users move to the new version simultaneously.
  • Rollback is instant — if something goes wrong after switching, you simply switch back to Blue. No partial states.
  • Testing style: The Green environment is fully tested before any real user traffic hits it.
  • Risk level: Low — but not zero. If a bug only appears under real production load, you won't catch it until all users are affected.
  • Cost: High — you are running two full environments simultaneously during the deployment window.
  • ✅ Best for: major releases, regulatory-sensitive systems where rollback must be instant and clean.

🐤 Canary Deployment

A Canary deployment (named after the "canary in a coal mine" — a small warning signal) sends a small percentage of real user traffic to the new version while the majority stays on the old one. You watch how real users behave on the new version before rolling it out to everyone.

  • Canary deployment uses gradual percentage-based traffic shifting — a small slice of users gets the new version first.
  • Rollback: Gradual or instant — if the canary group shows errors, redirect their traffic back to the old version.
  • Testing style: The new version is tested with real users and real production data — this catches bugs that staging environments miss.
  • Risk level: Very low — only a small fraction of users are affected if something goes wrong.
  • Cost: Medium — the new environment starts small and scales up gradually (aided by Auto Scaling).
  • ✅ Best for: small features, risky updates, when you want real-world validation before full rollout.

💡 In AWS, Canary deployments are implemented using Route 53 Weighted Routing — start with a 5% weight on the new environment, monitor, then gradually increase. This is exactly what the session's weighted routing diagram illustrated.

🎓 Exam Tips & Traps

  • If the question mentions random distribution of healthy IPs with no advanced routing logic → answer is Multivalue Answer, not Failover, not Latency.
  • If the question mentions user's geographic location → Geolocation. If it mentions lowest latency → Latency. These are different and commonly confused.
  • Failover routing requires health checks — if the exam says "automatically switch to backup when primary fails" → Failover with health check.
  • Simple Routing has no health check support — frequently tested.
  • Blue/Green costs more (two full environments). Canary costs less (new env starts small).
  • Route 53 port = 53. DNS TTL controls how long resolvers cache responses.
  • A public hosted zone routes internet traffic; a private hosted zone routes traffic within a VPC — know the difference.
  • RPO = data loss tolerance. RTO = downtime tolerance. Lower = better = more expensive.

📝 Overall Summary

  • RPO (Recovery Point Objective): How much data loss you can tolerate — controlled by backup/snapshot frequency. Lower RPO = more frequent backups.
  • RTO (Recovery Time Objective): How long your system can be down after disaster — controlled by recovery speed. Lower RTO = pre-warmed infrastructure.
  • Route 53 is AWS's DNS and traffic routing service with a 100% availability SLA, supporting domain registration, DNS resolution, health checking, and traffic routing.
  • DNS Resolution is a 9-step process involving the user, DNS resolver, root name server, TLD name server, and Route 53, before ultimately reaching the destination (e.g., CloudFront CDN).
  • Simple Routing sends all traffic to one resource and has no health check support.
  • Weighted Routing splits traffic by relative weights — perfect for canary deployments and A/B testing.
  • Geolocation Routing routes by user location; Latency Routing routes by best network performance — these are different.
  • Failover Routing is active-passive disaster recovery requiring health checks on the primary.
  • Multivalue Answer returns up to 8 healthy IPs randomly — lightweight DNS-level load balancing, not a replacement for a real load balancer.
  • IP-Based Routing routes traffic by client IP CIDR ranges.
  • Blue/Green = instant 100% switch, instant rollback, two full environments, higher cost. Best for major releases.
  • Canary = gradual percentage shift using weighted routing, real-user testing, lower cost. Best for risky or small updates.

Project 1: Create a Hosted Zone and Test DNS Resolution

🎯 Objective: Create a public hosted zone in Route 53, add an A record, and verify DNS resolution using dig/nslookup commands.

⏱️ Time: 20–30 min

💰 Cost: $0.50/month per hosted zone (outside Free Tier)

  1. Open the AWS Management Console and navigate to Route 53.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Route 53 Console — look for Hosted Zones in the left navigation

  2. Click Hosted zones → Create hosted zone. Enter your domain name, select Public hosted zone, and click Create hosted zone.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Create Hosted Zone form — confirm Public type is selected

  3. Note the four NS (Name Server) records that Route 53 automatically creates. You'll need to enter these at your domain registrar if you registered elsewhere.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: NS records — copy these four values to your registrar

  4. Click Create record. Set record type to A, enter your EC2 instance's public IP in the value field, set TTL to 300 seconds, and save.

  5. Open your terminal and run nslookup yourdomain.com or dig yourdomain.com to verify resolution returns your IP address.

Project 2: Configure Failover Routing with Health Checks

🎯 Objective: Set up a primary and secondary record with Failover routing and attach health checks to simulate disaster recovery.

⏱️ Time: 30–45 min

💰 Cost: $0.50/health check/month

💰 Cost Note: Health checks are billed at approximately $0.50/month each for AWS endpoints. Stop/delete them after the lab to avoid ongoing charges.

  1. In Route 53, click Health checks → Create health check. Set the endpoint type to IP address, enter your primary EC2's IP and port 80. Name it primary-health-check.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Health Check form — confirm endpoint IP and port 80

  2. Create a second health check for your secondary endpoint (a different EC2 instance or S3 static website).

  3. In your hosted zone, create a record: name = www, type = A, routing policy = Failover, failover record type = Primary, attach the primary health check, enter your primary EC2 IP.

  4. Create a second record: same name, type A, routing policy Failover, failover record type = Secondary, enter your secondary resource IP. Attach the secondary health check.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Failover records — confirm Primary and Secondary labels are visible

  5. Stop your primary EC2 instance. Wait 30–60 seconds, then run dig www.yourdomain.com. Verify the IP switches to your secondary resource.

  6. Cleanup: Delete both health checks, delete the failover records, and terminate any EC2 instances created for this project.

Project 3: Canary Deployment with Weighted Routing

🎯 Objective: Simulate a real canary deployment — run two versions of a static website side by side using Route 53 Weighted Routing, then gradually shift traffic from v1 to v2.

⏱️ Time: 30–45 min

💰 Cost: Free Tier (S3 static hosting) + $0.50/hosted zone/month

⚡ Why this matters: This is exactly how companies deploy new software without taking down production. You'll feel the difference between "big bang" releases and controlled rollouts.

  1. Create two S3 buckets with static website hosting enabled. Name them clearly — for example mysite-v1 and mysite-v2.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Two S3 buckets — v1 and v2 both visible in the bucket list

  2. Upload a simple index.html to each bucket with a visible version label. In v1 write: <h1>Version 1 — Stable</h1>. In v2 write: <h1>Version 2 — New Feature!</h1>. Make the background colours different (e.g., blue for v1, green for v2) so you can visually confirm which version loads.

  3. In your Route 53 hosted zone, create two CNAME or Alias records — both for the same subdomain (e.g., app.yourdomain.com). Set routing policy to Weighted. Record 1: points to v1 bucket website endpoint, weight = 90. Record 2: points to v2 bucket website endpoint, weight = 10.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Weighted records — confirm weights 90 and 10 are set correctly

  4. Open app.yourdomain.com in your browser and refresh 10 times. Note how many times you see v1 vs v2. You should see v2 roughly 1 in 10 times.

  5. Now simulate a successful canary phase: edit the weights to 50 / 50. Refresh again and observe roughly equal distribution.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: 50/50 split — both weights updated to 50

  6. Complete the cutover: set v1 weight to 0 and v2 weight to 100. Verify all traffic now reaches v2 only.

  7. Reflection: What would happen if v2 had a bug at the 10% stage? How quickly could you roll back? (Answer: set v2 weight back to 0 — instant, no redeployment needed.)

  8. Cleanup: Delete the weighted records, empty and delete both S3 buckets.

Project 4: Geolocation Routing — Serve Different Content by Region

🎯 Objective: Route users from different countries to different S3 static websites using Route 53 Geolocation routing — simulating a real multi-region content strategy.

⏱️ Time: 30–40 min

💰 Cost: Free Tier (S3) + Route 53 hosted zone

⚡ Real-world use: This is how companies serve localised content — different languages, different promotions, different legal disclaimers — without building separate apps.

  1. Create three S3 buckets with static website hosting. Name them: site-india, site-europe, site-default. Upload a different index.html to each — include a visible country label and background colour so you can tell them apart instantly.

  2. In your Route 53 hosted zone, create three records all for the same subdomain (e.g., geo.yourdomain.com), routing policy = Geolocation:

    • Record 1: Location = Asia (or India if available) → v1 bucket endpoint
    • Record 2: Location = Europe → v2 bucket endpoint
    • Record 3: Location = Default → default bucket endpoint (catches all other locations)

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Geolocation records — confirm all three location tags are visible

  3. Test using the Route 53 Test record set feature: go to your hosted zone → click Test record set (or use the Route 53 console checker). Enter a resolver IP from India (e.g., 49.207.0.0) and verify it returns the India bucket. Enter a European IP (e.g., 78.46.0.0) and verify it returns the Europe bucket.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Route 53 record test — shows which geolocation record matched and what IP was returned

  4. Open geo.yourdomain.com from your own browser and note which version loads — this reflects your actual geographic location as seen by Route 53.

  5. Key learning: Notice that the Default record is essential. Without it, users from unmatched locations would get a DNS resolution failure. Always set a default in geolocation routing.

  6. Cleanup: Delete all three geolocation records, empty and delete S3 buckets.

Project 5: Multivalue Answer Routing — DNS Load Distribution

🎯 Objective: Configure Multivalue Answer routing across three endpoints with health checks attached, observe how unhealthy endpoints are automatically excluded from DNS responses.

⏱️ Time: 35–50 min

💰 Cost: ~$1.50/month for 3 health checks (delete after lab)

💰 Cost Note: Each health check costs ~$0.50/month. Create all 3, run the lab, then delete immediately. Total cost if you run for 1 hour ≈ $0.002.

  1. Create three S3 buckets with static website hosting. Each index.html should display its server number clearly: "Server 1", "Server 2", "Server 3". Note down the website endpoint URLs for each.

  2. Create a Route 53 health check for each bucket's website endpoint. Set protocol to HTTP, port 80, path /index.html. Name them server-1-hc, server-2-hc, server-3-hc.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Three health checks — all green Healthy status visible

  3. In your hosted zone, create three A records all for the same subdomain (e.g., multi.yourdomain.com). Routing policy = Multivalue answer. Each record has a different IP (your S3 endpoint resolves to an IP — use dig bucket-name.s3-website-region.amazonaws.com to get it). Attach the corresponding health check to each record.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Multivalue records — confirm three records exist with health checks attached

  4. Run dig multi.yourdomain.com from your terminal several times. You should see up to 3 IPs returned in random order each time.

    # Run this 5 times and observe the order changes dig multi.yourdomain.com # Expected: ANSWER SECTION shows 2-3 IP addresses in varying order

  5. Now simulate a failure: go to one of the S3 buckets and delete the index.html file. Wait 30–60 seconds for the health check to detect the failure (check health check status in Route 53 console — it should turn red).

  6. Run dig multi.yourdomain.com again. Verify the failed endpoint's IP no longer appears in the DNS response — Route 53 has automatically removed it.

Project 6: Automate Route 53 with Boto3

🎯 Objective: Use Python + Boto3 to programmatically create a hosted zone, add DNS records, and list all records — moving from console clicks to code.

💰 Cost: $0.50/hosted zone created (delete after lab)

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Terminal output — Hosted Zone ID printed after successful creation

  3. Add a DNS A record to the hosted zone using Boto3:

    # Step 2: Create an A record client.change_resource_record_sets( HostedZoneId=hosted_zone_id, ChangeBatch={ 'Comment': 'Adding test A record', 'Changes': [{ 'Action': 'CREATE', 'ResourceRecordSet': { 'Name': 'www.mylab-test.example.com', 'Type': 'A', 'TTL': 300, 'ResourceRecords': [ {'Value': '203.0.113.10'} # test IP ] } }] } ) print("A record created successfully")

Project 7: Simulate a Full Disaster Recovery Failover (S3 Primary → S3 Backup)

🎯 Objective: Build a complete active-passive DR setup using two S3 static websites as primary and backup, with Route 53 Failover routing and health checks. Then simulate a real outage and watch traffic automatically shift.

⏱️ Time: 45–60 min

💰 Cost: ~$1.00/month for 2 health checks + hosted zone (delete after lab)

⚡ This project ties the whole session together: RPO (your backup S3 bucket IS your recovery point), RTO (how fast Route 53 detects failure and switches), and Failover routing policy all in one live demo.

  1. Create two S3 buckets with static website hosting: primary-site and backup-site. Upload a clearly labelled index.html to each. Primary should say "🟢 PRIMARY SITE — All systems normal". Backup should say "🔴 BACKUP SITE — Primary is down. You are on the backup."

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Two S3 buckets — primary-site and backup-site both visible in the list

  2. Create two Route 53 health checks — one for each bucket's website endpoint URL. Set protocol HTTP, port 80, path /index.html. Name them primary-hc and backup-hc. Wait until both show Healthy status (green) before proceeding.

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Both health checks green — confirm before creating routing records

  3. In your hosted zone, create two records for dr.yourdomain.com with routing policy Failover:

    • Record 1: Failover record type = Primary, value = primary-site endpoint, associate with primary-hc
    • Record 2: Failover record type = Secondary, value = backup-site endpoint, associate with backup-hc

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Failover records configured — Primary and Secondary clearly labeled with health checks attached

  4. Open dr.yourdomain.com in your browser. You should see "🟢 PRIMARY SITE". Confirm you're hitting the primary.

  5. Simulate the disaster: Go into the primary S3 bucket and delete index.html. This makes the bucket return a 403/404, which the health check will detect as a failure.

  6. Go to Route 53 → Health checks and watch primary-hc. Wait for it to turn red (typically 30–60 seconds based on your health check interval setting).

    Figure 📸 Screenshot: Primary health check turned red — failover should now be active

  7. Refresh dr.yourdomain.com in your browser (may need to wait for TTL to expire — set TTL to 60 seconds when creating records for faster testing). You should now see "🔴 BACKUP SITE — Primary is down."

  8. Measure your RTO: Record the time from when you deleted index.html to when the browser started returning the backup site. This is your observed RTO for this architecture.

    # Use dig to observe the DNS switch in real time watch -n 5 "dig dr.yourdomain.com +short" # Run every 5 seconds — watch the IP change when failover triggers

  9. Restore: Re-upload index.html to the primary bucket. Watch primary-hc turn green again. Traffic will automatically return to the primary.

  10. Cleanup: Delete failover records, delete both health checks, empty and delete both S3 buckets.

✅ Reflection: You just built and tested a real disaster recovery system. The same pattern — Route 53 Failover routing + health checks + a standby resource — is used in production systems serving millions of users. The only difference at scale is that EC2/ECS/ALB replaces S3, and the standby is in a different AWS region.

🤔 Reflection Questions

1. Your company's e-commerce database runs automated snapshots every 6 hours. A disaster strikes at 5:55 AM, just before the 6 AM snapshot. What is your effective RPO, and is this acceptable for an e-commerce platform?

💡 Hint

Think about how much transaction data could be lost in almost 6 hours of online shopping. Then consider whether RDS Multi-AZ or Aurora Global DB might be a better choice for this business.

2. A startup launches a new AI feature in their app. They want to test it with 5% of real users before rolling out to everyone. Which Route 53 routing policy should they use, and why not Failover?

💡 Hint

Think about what "5% of real users" implies about traffic distribution. Failover is for active-passive DR — it doesn't split traffic by percentage.

3. You have three EC2 instances in us-east-1, eu-west-1, and ap-southeast-1. A user in Singapore complains of slow load times. Which routing policy should you implement to give every user the fastest experience?

💡 Hint

Singapore is geographically close to ap-southeast-1, but "closest" doesn't always mean "fastest." Consider which policy measures actual network performance rather than geographic distance.

4. A company must ensure all European user data is processed only on EU servers due to GDPR requirements. Which routing policy do they need, and which one is NOT the right choice even though it sounds similar?

💡 Hint

The requirement is about where the user is, not about network speed. Compare Geolocation vs Latency routing and explain why location-based routing is the correct compliance answer here.

5. A development team running Blue/Green deployments complains their AWS bill has doubled during every release window. What is causing this, and what alternative deployment strategy would reduce costs while still minimising risk?

💡 Hint

Blue/Green requires two complete, full-size environments. Think about what makes Canary deployments cheaper and how Route 53 weighted routing enables this cost reduction.

6. Explain why Multivalue Answer routing is NOT a replacement for an Elastic Load Balancer. What can an ELB do that Multivalue routing cannot?

💡 Hint

Think about session stickiness, SSL termination, path-based routing, and Layer 7 awareness. Multivalue works at the DNS layer — it can only return IP addresses, nothing more.
