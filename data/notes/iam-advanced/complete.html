📖 Introduction

If IAM were a building, identity-based policies are your keycard — they say what you're allowed to do. But what stops a junior admin from accidentally giving someone too much access? What stops a shared vendor from peeking at the wrong customer's files? And how do you make sure that even if someone steals your login session, they can't delete your production database?

Those are exactly the problems this session solves. We're covering the advanced layer of IAM — the mechanisms that sit above ordinary identity policies and enforce hard limits, scoped assumptions, and real-time security decisions.

🔁 Quick Recap — How IAM Makes Decisions

Before diving into permission boundaries and session policies, it's critical to lock in the rule that underlies every IAM decision. Chandra called this "one of the few things you just have to memorize" — and for good reason. Every scenario question on the exam reduces to applying this rule correctly.

Figure 2: IAM's three-step authorization evaluation — the invariant rule that applies everywhere

Every IAM authorization decision follows the same three-step evaluation, regardless of which AWS service or how many policies are applied.

  1. Explicit Deny: If any evaluated policy contains an explicit deny, the request is denied immediately and evaluation stops. No allow, no condition, no other consideration can override an explicit deny.
  2. Explicit Allow: If an explicit allow exists and no deny overrides it, the request is allowed.
  3. Implicit Deny: If neither an explicit deny nor an explicit allow applies, the request is denied by default. AWS is deny-by-default — the absence of a permission means no permission.

⚡ The sentence to memorize: "Explicit deny wins. Explicit allow grants. Implicit deny is the default."

This rule is invariant. It applies to identity-based vs resource-based policy combinations, permission boundaries, service control policies, session policies, and cross-account evaluation. When an allow doesn't work during debugging, look for the explicit deny — it's always in one of the evaluated policies.

🔒 Permission Boundaries — The Standing Ceiling

Permission boundaries are, as Chandra put it, "one of the most misunderstood features in IAM." So let's be precise.

What a Boundary Does (and Doesn't Do)

A permission boundary grants nothing. It defines the maximum — the ceiling — of what an identity-based policy is allowed to grant to a user or role.

Think of it like a governor on a car engine. The engine (identity policy) might be capable of 200 mph. But the governor (boundary) limits it to 80 mph, no matter what. The governor doesn't make the car go — it just caps how fast it can go.

Figure 3: Effective permissions = intersection of identity policy and permission boundary

How It Resolves — Intersection

Effective permissions are the intersection of the identity-based policy and the permission boundary — only actions that both allow at the same time.

  • Identity policy grants 10 actions + boundary allows 3 of them → principal gets 3 actions
  • Identity policy grants AdministratorAccess + boundary allows only Amazon S3 → principal can do S3 only
  • Boundary has only Deny statements → the allowed set in the boundary is empty, so the intersection collapses to nothing — the principal can do essentially nothing regardless of the identity policy

❌ Common trap: Adding Deny statements to a permission boundary. A boundary works by intersection of allows. If you only put denies in the boundary, there's nothing in the allowed set — the intersection with any identity policy is empty. To use a boundary correctly, put the allowed actions in it.

The Delegated Administration Pattern

Here's the real reason permission boundaries exist. Imagine you want junior administrators to be able to attach policies to developer users — but you want to guarantee that those developers can never gain access to things outside an approved set.

You attach a permission boundary with the approved actions to each developer IAM user. Now it doesn't matter how broad a policy the junior admin attaches. The boundary caps the result automatically — and the security team doesn't need to review each policy attachment by hand.

✅ Exam tip from Chandra: "A permission boundary is an advanced IAM feature that sets the maximum permissions an identity-based policy can grant to an IAM user or role. Any action outside the boundary is implicitly denied no matter what an attached policy grants. The security team does not have to review each attachment because the boundary enforces the ceiling automatically."

⏱️ Session Policies — The Per-Call Scope

If permission boundaries are the standing ceiling on an entity, session policies are the temporary scope you apply to just one specific role assumption. These two are so often confused that Chandra called "keeping the two apart worth real exam points."

How Session Policies Work

A session policy is a policy you pass as a parameter at the moment you call STS:AssumeRole. It applies to that single session only — the role itself is unchanged.

Everyone else who assumes the same role is unaffected. You've simply narrowed this one assumption.

Figure 4: Session policies narrow one assumption — the role's permissions are unchanged for other callers

Resolution — Intersection Again

The effective permissions of the session are the intersection of the role's permission policy and the session policy you pass.

A hard rule: a session policy can only narrow, never widen. You cannot use a session policy to grant more than the role already allows.

⚡ Example: Role permits S3:GetObject and S3:PutObject. Session policy permits S3:GetObject and S3:DeleteObject. The session can only do S3:GetObject — the only action both allow.

Boundary vs Session Policy — The Clean Division

Feature Permission Boundary Session Policy
Where it lives Attached to an entity (user or role) Passed as parameter at AssumeRole time
Duration Permanent — applies across all sessions One session only
Affects whom Every session of that entity Only the caller who passes it
Use case Delegated admin ceiling, standing cap SaaS per-customer scoping, per-call narrowing
Resolution Intersection of allows Intersection of allows

✅ Decision rule: Use a session policy when you want to scope one specific assumption at the moment you make it. Use a permission boundary when you want a durable cap on an entity across everything it does in the system.

❌ Remember: An explicit deny in any policy — the identity policy, the resource policy, the boundary, or the session policy — still wins outright. Both session policies and permission boundaries work by intersection of allows. A deny anywhere in the picture overrides all of them.

🔄 Role Assumption — The Most-Missed IAM Fact

Chandra called this "the most important and most frequently missed fact about role assumption." It has a direct and surprising consequence for how you design permission boundaries — and the exam loves to test it.

What Drops at Assumption Time

When a principal assumes a role, the principal's own identity policies and permission boundaries are not evaluated for the session — they are set aside entirely.

The session runs with the role's permission policy and the role's own permission boundary only.

Figure 5: The caller's policies are dropped at assumption time — only the role's policies govern the session

The Three-Step Design Rule

  1. User policies drop away when the AssumeRole call succeeds. The identity policies and boundary on the calling principal stop mattering for the session.
  2. The role's policies govern. The session is authorized by the role's permission policy and the role's own boundary (or a session policy passed at assume time).
  3. Design rule: To limit what anyone gains by assuming a role, put the boundary on the role, not on the assuming user.

❌ The classic trap: Imagine you let developers assume a read-only analyst role. You try to cap them by putting a permission boundary on each developer user. Once they assume the role, the user's boundary is not evaluated — they get the role's full permissions. The correct control is a boundary on the role, which caps every session regardless of who assumes it.

⚡ Cross-account note: A permission boundary you put on a user in Account B does not follow that user into an assumed role session in Account A. It limits only what the user does directly with its own identity in Account B. This was hammered throughout both sessions — the policies and boundaries of the caller exist only in their own account and their own context.

🌐 Cross-Account Access — The Two-Policy Handshake

Cross-account access is one of the most frequently tested patterns on the SAA-C03 exam, and one of the most commonly answered incorrectly under time pressure. Chandra spent significant time on this across both sessions — including a live exercise to identify source vs destination account.

The Classic Scenario

A company has two AWS accounts. EC2 instances in Account B need to access encrypted videos stored in an S3 bucket in Account A. The S3 bucket is encrypted with a customer-managed KMS key also in Account A.

Figure 6: Cross-account architecture — EC2 in Account B assumes a role in Account A to access KMS-encrypted S3 objects

The Two-Policy Handshake

Cross-account access requires both policies simultaneously: the caller in the source account must have STS:AssumeRole permission, AND the role in the destination account must have a trust policy that names the caller.

⚡ Source vs Destination — get this right every time:

  • Source account = where the call originates = Account B (EC2 is here)
  • Destination account = where the resource and role live = Account A (S3 and KMS are here)

The trust policy lives in the destination account. The STS:AssumeRole permission lives in the source account. This is "one of those things you get wrong under exam pressure" — get it into your head now.

Step by Step

  1. Account A (destination): Create an IAM role with a trust policy allowing Account B to assume it. Attach a permission policy allowing s3:GetObject on the bucket and kms:Decrypt on the KMS key. Update the KMS key policy to independently allow the role to perform kms:Decrypt.
  2. Account B (source): Attach an instance profile to EC2 that allows sts:AssumeRole on Account A's role.
  3. At runtime: EC2 calls STS AssumeRole → STS issues temporary credentials → EC2 uses them to call s3:GetObject and kms:Decrypt.
  4. From IAM's perspective: Once the role is assumed, the session is treated as a same-account call by the assumed role identity in Account A. The cross-account aspect is resolved at assumption time.

KMS Two-Pronged Authorization

This is one of the highest-frequency exam patterns and highest-error production patterns. When you access a KMS-encrypted resource cross-account:

Both the IAM role's permission policy AND the KMS key's resource policy must independently permit kms:Decrypt.

❌ Exam distractor pattern: Answer options that mention only one of the two authorizations (e.g., "add KMS:Decrypt to the IAM role policy") are almost always wrong. You must have it in both places. This applies in same-account scenarios too — cross-account just makes it visible.

When Cross-Account Access Fails — Four Places to Check

When a cross-account call to a KMS-encrypted S3 bucket fails with Access Denied, check in this order:

  1. Principal's IAM policy in the source account (Account B) — does it have STS:AssumeRole for the target role?
  2. Role's trust policy in the destination account (Account A) — does it name the calling principal?
  3. Bucket policy in the destination account — any explicit deny?
  4. KMS key policy in the destination account — does it independently allow KMS:Decrypt for the role?

📚 From AWS documentation: When cross-account principals access KMS-encrypted resources, authorization is a two-stage process. The IAM principal must have permission to call KMS on the key (identity-side), and the KMS key policy must grant that principal permission to use the key (resource-side). Either side alone is insufficient.

📚 Source: AWS KMS Key Policy Documentation

🕵️ The Confused Deputy Problem & External ID

This is an advanced but exam-tested concept that arises specifically in third-party cross-account access (SaaS vendor scenarios). The exam combines it with session policy questions in "select two" format.

What is the Confused Deputy Problem?

Imagine a SaaS vendor (like a billing analytics platform) that reads data from each customer's AWS account. The vendor assumes a customer-provided IAM role in each customer account.

The risk: if the vendor's platform gets confused about which customer it's serving — or if a malicious customer passes a different customer's role ARN — the vendor's platform could access Customer A's data while acting on behalf of Customer B. The vendor itself becomes the "confused deputy" — it has legitimate permissions in both accounts, but gets tricked into misusing them.

Figure 7: The Confused Deputy attack — Customer B tricks the vendor into accessing Customer A's data

The Solution — External ID in the Trust Policy

The AWS-documented solution to the Confused Deputy problem is the STS External ID condition in the role's trust policy.

Here's how it works:

  1. The vendor generates a unique identifier per customer (e.g., a UUID). The vendor owns and controls this value.
  2. The customer adds a condition to their role's trust policy: sts:ExternalId must equal that specific UUID.
  3. On every AssumeRole call the vendor makes for that customer, it passes the customer's unique External ID.
  4. Even if a different customer provides the same role ARN, the assumption fails — the External ID doesn't match.

✅ Why aws:SourceIp doesn't solve this: An IP restriction limits where calls come from, but all customer assumptions originate from the same vendor platform IP range. Both Customer A and Customer B's roles might trust the vendor's IP range — the vendor could still accidentally assume Customer A's role while processing Customer B. Source IP solves where, not who for. External ID solves which customer.

🔐 MFA Condition Keys — Present vs Age

AWS has two IAM policy condition keys involving MFA. They look similar, but they solve completely different problems — and this distinction is directly tested on the SAA-C03 exam.

Figure 8: MultiFactorAuthPresent stays true all session; MultiFactorAuthAge grows with time and enforces freshness

aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent

aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent is a Boolean that returns true if the session was authenticated with MFA at any point during the session's lifetime. It is set at session start and does not change as the session ages.

A session that signed in with MFA six hours ago still has MultiFactorAuthPresent = true.

❌ Security gap: MultiFactorAuthPresent does NOT protect against session hijacking. If an attacker steals a session token hours after sign-in, the condition is still true — the attacker has the same access as the legitimate user for the rest of the session.

aws:MultiFactorAuthAge

aws:MultiFactorAuthAge is a numeric value in seconds measuring the elapsed time since the MFA credential was issued. It increases as the session ages.

You compare it using NumericLessThan. For example: aws:MultiFactorAuthAge: 900 means "MFA was performed within the last 15 minutes."

✅ Session hijacking defense: MultiFactorAuthAge with a small threshold forces the user to re-authenticate with MFA before performing sensitive operations. A hijacked session will eventually fail this condition as the age exceeds the threshold — blocking destructive actions even in a stolen session.

Production Architecture Pattern

The best practice Chandra described:

  • Routine read operations: Use aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent: true — session-level MFA at sign-in is sufficient. No friction for daily work.
  • Destructive operations (delete, modify production): Use aws:MultiFactorAuthAge with a small threshold (e.g., NumericLessThan: 900) — requires fresh MFA within 15 minutes.

⚡ One line to remember: "Present is for the session. Age is for the action."

Feature aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent aws:MultiFactorAuthAge
Type Boolean Numeric (seconds)
Changes over time? No — stays true all session Yes — increases every second
Protects against hijacking? No Yes (with small threshold)
Use for Read-only operations Destructive/sensitive operations
Comparison operator Bool: true/false NumericLessThan: 900
Exam trigger phrase "MFA somewhere in the session" "Fresh MFA", "session hijacking", "last X seconds"

📱 AWS MFA Mechanisms

AWS supports four MFA mechanisms for IAM users and the root user. Each has different operational characteristics and resistance to specific attack patterns — these distinctions appear on the exam.

Figure 9: The four AWS MFA mechanisms compared by type, security properties, and weaknesses

1. Virtual MFA (TOTP App)

A time-based one-time password app on a phone (Google Authenticator, Authy, Duo Mobile). Fast to enroll, free, low friction. Weakness: relies on phone security and is not phishing-resistant.

2. Hardware MFA Token

A physical time-based key device (SafeID, AWS-shipped tokens). No phone dependency, tamper-resistant hardware. Weakness: hardware procurement cost and the same phishing vulnerability as virtual MFA — someone can still present a fake login page to intercept the code.

3. FIDO Security Key

A hardware key using public key cryptography (Google Titan, YubiKey). FIDO security keys are phishing-resistant because the credential is cryptographically bound to the original domain — it cannot be replayed against a different domain. Weakness: hardware procurement and user training for the tap-to-authenticate flow.

4. Passkey

A FIDO2 device-bound or syncable credential (Touch ID, Windows Hello, password manager passkeys). Phishing-resistant, smooth UX. Supported by IAM Identity Center. Weakness: newer pattern, sync model varies by platform, some legacy flows don't support it.

✅ 2026 production recommendation: Virtual MFA for general engineering staff. FIDO security keys or passkeys for high-privileged identities (root users, security administrators).

❌ SMS MFA was deprecated in 2019. New SMS MFA configurations cannot be created. Existing configurations remain functional but AWS enforces migration in new projects.

🏗️ The Full IAM Security Stack — Four Mechanisms

Chandra closed Session 6 with a unifying mental model that covers roughly 80% of IAM questions on the exam. Four mechanisms, each with a specific role:

Figure 10: The four-layer IAM security stack — floor, ceiling, gate, and contextual gates

Mechanism Role Metaphor When to Use
Identity-based policies Floor Job description — what the identity can do Grant baseline permissions to users/groups/roles
Permission boundaries Ceiling Governor on an engine — caps maximum speed Delegated admin, limit blast radius when teams write their own policies
Resource-based policies Gate Building access list — the resource decides who enters Absolute denies, cross-account access without role assumption
Condition keys Contextual gates Time-lock or location-aware entry system Source IP, MFA presence, MFA age, time of day, request tags, region

⚡ Exam strategy: When a scenario question describes multiple security requirements, map each requirement to exactly one mechanism. The correct answer is almost always a combination — e.g., "permission boundary for the delegation requirement + session policy for the per-call scoping requirement." If you can identify each requirement → mechanism mapping, "select two" questions become much easier.

📝 Knowledge Check Walkthrough

Chandra used two scenario questions in these sessions that demonstrate exactly how the exam structures multi-requirement, multi-mechanism questions. Let's walk through both with the reasoning.

Knowledge Check 1 — Delegated Administration Boundary

Scenario: A security team manages an AWS account where delegated administrators create IAM users for developers and attach identity-based policies. The security team wants to limit developer permissions to S3, EC2, and DynamoDB only — and ensure delegated admins cannot grant permissions outside the approved set, even by attaching broad managed policies. No manual policy review.

Correct answer: Attach a permission boundary allowing only the approved actions to each developer IAM user.

Why it works: Regardless of how broad a policy a junior admin attaches, the boundary caps the result. The security team defines the allowed set once in the boundary, and that ceiling is automatically enforced on every policy attachment.

Why the other options fail:

  • Option B (attach the approved policy as identity policy): Instructing admins "don't attach broader policies" is not a technical enforcement. A mistake or malicious action can still broaden access.
  • Option C (explicit deny for all disallowed actions): Listing every disallowed AWS action is impractical — there are thousands of AWS actions. A boundary listing only the allowed set is far more maintainable.
  • Option D (boundary with explicit denies on approved actions): This is backwards — it would deny the approved actions. Boundaries must list what IS allowed, not what's denied.

Knowledge Check 2 — Confused Deputy + Per-Call Scoping (Select Two)

Scenario: A SaaS vendor reads billing S3 objects from each customer's account. A new enterprise customer requires: (1) vendor's role assumption cannot be tricked into accessing one customer's data while acting for a different customer, and (2) a single role assumption is limited to read access on a specific S3 prefix, even if the role's permission policy is broader.

Correct answers: B and D

  • B — STS External ID in the trust policy: The vendor generates a unique value per customer and passes it on every AssumeRole call. The customer requires this exact value. Even if a different customer passes the same role ARN, the assumption fails — the External ID doesn't match. This solves the Confused Deputy requirement.
  • D — Session policy scoped to specific bucket and prefix: A session policy passed as a parameter to STS:AssumeRole narrows the resulting session's effective permissions to exactly the per-customer S3 prefix, even though the role's permission policy is broader. This solves the per-call scoping requirement.

Why the other options fail:

  • A (aws:SourceIp): Both customers' roles might trust the vendor's IP range — source IP doesn't identify which customer's call it is.
  • C (short duration_seconds): Limits credential lifetime but does nothing to prevent accessing the wrong customer's data.
  • E (permission boundary on the role): A boundary applies across all assumptions of the role — it cannot be scoped per-customer per-assumption. Session policy is the correct mechanism for per-assumption scoping.

🎯 SAA-C03 Exam Tips

These are the patterns Chandra highlighted as the most tested — and most often answered incorrectly — in the SAA-C03 exam.

⚡ Tip 1 — Permission boundary is a positive construct: It lists what IS allowed (maximum permissions), not what's forbidden. A boundary with only deny statements = empty allowed set = no effective permissions.

⚡ Tip 2 — Boundary on the role, not the user: A user's permission boundary is NOT evaluated during an assumed role session. Always put the cap on the role if you want it to apply to everyone who assumes it.

⚡ Tip 3 — Session policy = per-call; boundary = standing: Session policies apply to one assumption. Boundaries apply to the entity across all sessions. "Select two" questions that combine both often mean: External ID for Confused Deputy + session policy for per-call scoping.

⚡ Tip 4 — Explicit deny wins everywhere: Regardless of policy type (identity, resource, boundary, session, SCP) and regardless of account (same or cross-account), an explicit deny overrides any allow. When an allow doesn't work, find the deny.

⚡ Tip 5 — Source vs destination account: Source = where the call originates (EC2 lives here). Destination = where the resource and role live. The STS:AssumeRole permission is in the source account. The trust policy, bucket policy, and KMS key policy are in the destination account.

⚡ Tip 6 — KMS needs two-pronged authorization: Cross-account KMS access requires BOTH the IAM role policy AND the KMS key policy to permit KMS:Decrypt. Answer options mentioning only one are usually distractors.

⚡ Tip 7 — MFA Age vs Present: "Fresh MFA", "session hijacking", "last X seconds" → aws:MultiFactorAuthAge with NumericLessThan. "MFA somewhere in the session" → aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent.

⚡ Tip 8 — SMS MFA was deprecated in 2019 — new configurations cannot be created. If an answer suggests enabling SMS MFA as a new security control, it's wrong.

📋 Six Key Takeaways (Chandra's Closing List)

Chandra ended Session 6 with six points he said "cover the IAM exam questions you will encounter." Internalize all six:

  1. Roles are assumed, not used. A role has no long-lived credentials. A trusted principal assumes it and receives temporary credentials from STS.
  2. Workloads get roles. EC2 via instance profile. Lambda via execution role. AWS managed services via service-linked roles.
  3. Lambda has two controls. The execution role says what the function may do. The resource-based policy says who may invoke it. Do not confuse the two.
  4. Cross-account access is a two-policy handshake. The caller needs STS:AssumeRole permission. The role needs a trust policy naming the caller. Both are required.
  5. Boundaries cap, session policies scope. A permission boundary is a standing ceiling on an entity. A session policy narrows one assumption. Both work by intersection of allows.
  6. Cap the role, not the assuming user. The role session runs with the role's policies and the role's boundary — not the assumer's. External ID in the trust policy stops the Confused Deputy.

📝 Overall Summary
  • Permission boundaries: A ceiling attached to an IAM entity (user/role). Lists maximum allowed actions (positive construct). Effective permissions = intersection of identity policy and boundary. Never grants anything on its own.
  • Session policies: Passed as a parameter to STS:AssumeRole. Applies to one session only. Can only narrow, never widen beyond what the role allows. Effective permissions = intersection of role policy and session policy.
  • Critical distinction: Boundary = durable standing cap on entity across all sessions. Session policy = per-call scope for one assumption only.
  • Role assumption drops the caller's policies: When a principal assumes a role, the caller's identity policies and permission boundaries are set aside. Only the role's own policies and boundary govern the session.
  • Design rule: To cap what anyone gains by assuming a role, put the boundary on the role — not on the assuming user.
  • Cross-account two-policy handshake: STS:AssumeRole permission in the source account + trust policy naming the caller in the destination account. Both required.
  • Source account = where the call originates. Destination account = where the role and resource live.
  • KMS two-pronged authorization: Cross-account KMS-encrypted resource access requires BOTH the IAM role policy AND the KMS key policy to independently permit KMS:Decrypt.
  • Confused Deputy prevention: Use STS External ID in the trust policy — a unique per-customer value the vendor passes on every AssumeRole call.
  • aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent = Boolean, set at session start, true all session, not phishing/hijack resistant. Use for read-only operations.
  • aws:MultiFactorAuthAge = Numeric seconds since MFA, increases over time. Use NumericLessThan: 900 for fresh MFA on destructive operations. Defends against session hijacking.
  • Explicit deny wins everywhere: In any policy type, same or cross-account — explicit deny overrides any allow. The IAM evaluation rule is invariant.

Project 1: Implement a Permission Boundary for Delegated Administration

🎯 Objective: Create a permission boundary that limits a developer user to S3 read-only, then verify a junior admin cannot grant broader permissions by attaching a wider policy.

⏱️ Time: 30–45 min

💰 Cost: Free tier eligible

  1. Sign in to the AWS Console and navigate to IAM → Policies. Click Create policy. Use the JSON editor to create a boundary policy allowing only s3:GetObject and s3:ListBucket on all resources. Name it DeveloperPermissionBoundary.

    📸 Screenshot: IAM Policy JSON editor with boundary policy — verify both S3 actions are listed under Allow

  2. Navigate to IAM → Users → Create user. Name the user developer-test. On the permissions step, do not attach any policies yet. Click through to create.

  3. Open the developer-test user. Under Permissions boundary, click Set boundary. Select DeveloperPermissionBoundary and confirm.

    📸 Screenshot: Setting the permission boundary on the developer-test user

  4. Now attach AdministratorAccess managed policy to developer-test. (This simulates a junior admin over-granting access.)

  5. Test by switching to the developer-test credentials and attempting to call ec2:DescribeInstances — it should fail, because EC2 is outside the boundary. Then call s3:ListBuckets — it should succeed.

  6. Cleanup: Delete the developer-test user and the DeveloperPermissionBoundary policy.

Project 2: Cross-Account Role Assumption with STS (Simulated in One Account)

🎯 Objective: Simulate cross-account role assumption using two IAM users in the same account — one as the "source" caller and one representing the "destination" role. Verify the two-policy handshake.

⏱️ Time: 30–45 min

💰 Cost: Free tier eligible

  1. Create an IAM role named CrossAccountSimRole. In the trust policy, set the trusted principal to your AWS account ID (simulating Account B trusting Account A's structure). Attach a permission policy granting s3:ListBuckets only.

    📸 Screenshot: Trust policy editor — verify the Principal is your account ID and action is sts:AssumeRole

  2. Create an IAM user named source-caller with no permissions initially. Generate access keys.

  3. Try calling sts:AssumeRole for CrossAccountSimRole using the AWS CLI with source-caller credentials. It should fail with Access Denied — the user has no sts:AssumeRole permission yet.

  4. Attach an inline policy to source-caller granting sts:AssumeRole on the role ARN. Try again — it should now succeed and return temporary credentials.

  5. Use the temporary credentials to call s3:ListBuckets — it should succeed. Try s3:PutObject — it should fail (not in the role's permission policy). This demonstrates the handshake and role-governed session.

    📸 Screenshot: STS assume-role returns AccessKeyId, SecretAccessKey, and SessionToken

  6. Cleanup: Delete source-caller user, delete CrossAccountSimRole role.

🤔 Reflection Questions

1. You want junior admins to create policies for developers but guarantee developers can never exceed an approved set. Why does putting the boundary on the developer user work — and does it still work if the developer later assumes a role?

💡 Hint

Think about what gets evaluated during a direct user action vs. during an assumed role session. Where should the boundary live to be effective in both cases?

2. A SaaS vendor's platform is trusted by 50 customers. Each customer has a role with the same trust policy allowing the vendor. How does External ID prevent Customer B from accidentally (or maliciously) triggering access to Customer A's data?

💡 Hint

Consider what unique information is included in each customer's trust policy, and what the vendor must pass on every AssumeRole call for each specific customer.

3. A security alert fires: a production S3 deletion happened 6 hours after a user's MFA sign-in. Your policy uses aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent. How would switching to aws:MultiFactorAuthAge have prevented this?

💡 Hint

Think about what MultiFactorAuthPresent's value is 6 hours after sign-in vs. what MultiFactorAuthAge's value would be, and which comparator you'd use for a 15-minute freshness window.

4. You're designing a KMS-encrypted S3 cross-account access pattern. A colleague says "I've added KMS:Decrypt to the IAM role policy, so we're done." What critical step did they miss, and why does AWS require it?

💡 Hint

KMS uses two separate authorization checks — one on the identity side and one on the resource side. Both must independently permit the action.

5. In a cross-account scenario, you keep getting confused about which account is the source and which is the destination. What's the rule, and why does it matter for where you put the STS:AssumeRole permission vs the trust policy?

💡 Hint

Source = where the call originates. Destination = where the resource and role live. Each policy lives on the side where it makes sense — the caller needs permission to call, the role needs to declare who it trusts.

6. What is the mental model for when to use a permission boundary vs a session policy? Give a concrete real-world example for each.

💡 Hint

Think about whether the control needs to be permanent across all sessions (standing ceiling) or needs to vary per call (per-assumption narrowing). SaaS per-customer scoping vs delegated admin ceiling.
