🏛️ 1. AWS Well-Architected Framework (WAF)

Imagine you just moved into a new house 🏠. The construction is done, but now you need to make sure everything runs properly — electricity, plumbing, security alarms, energy efficiency. Moving in was just the first step!

That's exactly what the AWS Well-Architected Framework (WAF) is a set of best practices to design, build, and review workloads on AWS. It's not a service you turn on — it's a framework, a guide that helps you after you've already set things up in AWS.

💡 What problems does WAF solve? After companies migrate to AWS, they often face unexpected issues: huge bills at month-end, security breaches, no disaster recovery plan, poor performance. The WAF gives them a structured checklist to continuously review and improve their cloud setup.

Think of WAF as a quality audit system — like how hospitals have periodic inspections to ensure they're following all best practices. WAF lets you inspect your AWS architecture against industry standards.

Figure 1: The 6 Pillars of the AWS Well-Architected Framework

✅ Remember: WAF is NOT a service — it is a framework for technical correctness of your AWS workloads. It helps you after migration.

🏗️ 2. The 6 Pillars of WAF

The WAF is built on 6 pillars. Think of these as the 6 departments of a well-run company. Each focuses on a different area of your cloud health.

🖥️ Pillar 1: Operational Excellence

What it means: Running and monitoring systems to deliver business value and continually improving processes and procedures.

Real-world analogy: Think of a restaurant kitchen. Operational Excellence means the kitchen has clear workflows, chefs know their roles, orders are tracked, and the head chef regularly reviews what went wrong to improve next time.

In AWS terms: Automate deployments, monitor with CloudWatch, run frequent small changes rather than big risky ones, anticipate failures and respond to them.

⚡ Key question to ask: "Are we running our systems efficiently AND learning from every incident?"

🔒 Pillar 2: Security

What it means: Protecting your information systems from unauthorized access, data breaches, and cyber attacks.

Real-world analogy: Security is like the lock on your front door, the CCTV camera outside, and the safe inside your bedroom. Multiple layers of protection.

In AWS terms: Use IAM for access control, encrypt data at rest and in transit, enable CloudTrail for audit logs, use Security Groups and NACLs for network access control.

✅ Security covers: Identity management, detecting threats, protecting data, maintaining compliance.

🔄 Pillar 3: Reliability

What it means: Ensuring a workload performs its intended function correctly and consistently when it's expected to.

Real-world analogy: A reliable car starts every time you turn the key, even in rain or cold weather. Reliability in cloud means your app keeps working even when one server crashes.

In AWS terms: Use Auto Scaling so your app handles traffic spikes, Multi-AZ deployments so one data center failure doesn't take you down, backups with S3 and RDS snapshots.

⚡ Key question: "Can my system recover automatically from failures?"

⚡ Pillar 4: Performance Efficiency

What it means: Using IT and compute resources efficiently — getting maximum output from minimum input.

Real-world analogy: A fuel-efficient car gets more kilometres per litre. Performance efficiency in cloud means you're not over-provisioning (paying for resources you don't use) or under-provisioning (slow performance).

In AWS terms: Choose the right EC2 instance type for your workload, use serverless (Lambda) where appropriate, use CloudFront CDN to serve content closer to users.

✅ Performance efficiency is about the right resource at the right time.

💰 Pillar 5: Cost Optimization

What it means: Avoiding unnecessary costs by understanding and controlling where money is being spent.

Real-world analogy: Turning off lights when you leave a room. In cloud, this means shutting down unused EC2 instances, deleting old EBS snapshots, right-sizing your infrastructure.

In AWS terms: Use Reserved Instances for predictable workloads, Spot Instances for flexible jobs, AWS Cost Explorer to track spending, S3 lifecycle policies to move old data to cheaper storage.

⚡ Key question: "Am I paying for anything I don't actually need?"

🌱 Pillar 6: Sustainability

What it means: Minimizing the environmental impacts of running cloud workloads.

Real-world analogy: Choosing a hybrid car over a petrol-only car to reduce your carbon footprint. In cloud, this means reducing energy consumption.

In AWS terms: Use managed services (less infrastructure to manage), select regions powered by renewable energy, optimize code to run faster with less compute.

✅ Sustainability was the newest addition to WAF pillars — added in 2021. Expect a question about it!
📝 Memory Trick for 6 Pillars: OSRPCS — Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, Sustainability. Or remember: "Our Security Really Performs Cost Sustainably" 🎯

🗺️ 3. AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF)

Now here's a different scenario: Your company has decided to move to the cloud, but you haven't started yet. You're asking, "Are we even READY to migrate?" That's where the CAF comes in.

The AWS Cloud Adoption Framework (CAF) helps organizations understand how to prepare for cloud migration — it is a framework for organizational readiness.

💡 Key Insight: Moving to the cloud is NOT just a technology change — it's an organisational change. You need to train people, update processes, change how teams work, and ensure security compliance. CAF addresses all of these.

Think of it like relocating your entire office to a new city. You don't just pack computers — you need HR to manage employee transitions, Finance to budget the move, IT to set up the new network, and Management to communicate the strategy. CAF covers all these angles.

Figure 2: The 6 Perspectives of the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework

🔭 4. The 6 Perspectives of CAF

CAF has 6 perspectives — not "pillars" like WAF, but "perspectives." Each perspective represents a different viewpoint or stakeholder group.

💼 Perspective 1: Business

Focus: Business support capabilities to optimize business value with cloud adoption.

Who's involved: Business Managers, Finance Managers, Budget Owners, Strategy Stakeholders.

What they're asking: "How does moving to cloud align with our business goals? What's the ROI? How do we justify this investment to the board?"

⚡ Business perspective ensures cloud migration serves actual business outcomes, not just IT goals.

👥 Perspective 2: People

Focus: People development, training, communications, and change management.

Who's involved: Human Resources, Staffing, People Managers.

What they're asking: "Do our employees have the skills to work in a cloud environment? How do we train them? How do we manage the cultural shift?"

✅ This is often overlooked — technology changes fail when people aren't prepared. CAF makes sure human factors are addressed.

📊 Perspective 3: Governance

Focus: Managing and measuring resulting business outcomes.

Who's involved: CIO, Program Managers, Project Managers, Enterprise Architects, Business Analysts, Portfolio Managers.

What they're asking: "How do we track our cloud spending? How do we ensure compliance? What KPIs measure cloud success?"

⚡ Governance is about control and measurement — making sure the migration stays on track and delivers expected value.

🖥️ Perspective 4: Platform

Focus: Develop, maintain, and optimize cloud solutions and services.

Who's involved: CTO, IT Managers, Solution Architects.

What they're asking: "How do we architect our applications for the cloud? What services do we use? How do we design our VPCs and networking?"

✅ Platform perspective is most technical — it's about the actual cloud infrastructure design and implementation.

⚙️ Perspective 5: Operations

Focus: Allows system health and reliability through the move to the cloud, and delivers an agile cloud computing operation.

Who's involved: IT Operations Managers, IT Support Managers.

What they're asking: "How do we monitor our cloud systems? How do we handle incidents? How do we maintain uptime in the cloud?"

⚡ Operations perspective ensures day-to-day cloud management is smooth and reliable after migration.

🔐 Perspective 6: Security

Focus: Ensures that the workloads deployed or developed in the cloud align to the organization's security control, resiliency, and compliance requirements.

Who's involved: CISO, IT Security Managers, IT Security Analysts, Head of Audit and Compliance.

What they're asking: "Are our cloud workloads secure? Do we meet compliance requirements like GDPR, HIPAA? How do we handle data protection?"

✅ Security in CAF is about organizational compliance — making sure the migration follows security policies and regulations.
📝 Memory Trick for 6 Perspectives: BGPPOS — Business, Governance, People, Platform, Operations, Security. Or: "Big Governments Prefer Proper Operational Security" 🎯

🔐 6. Why Hardening is Needed

Here's a fundamental truth of security: Security is about controlling access.

Let's use an analogy. Imagine you have a gold bar 🥇 worth ₹50 lakhs. How do you protect it? You don't just leave it on the table! You put it in a locker → inside a cupboard → inside a locked bedroom → inside a secured house with a gate → with CCTV cameras watching who enters. Multiple layers of protection, both before someone reaches the gold and after.

In the digital world, we protect servers and data the same way. There are two places where access can be controlled:

  • Before someone reaches the system → This is Network Hardening
  • After someone reaches the system → This is System Hardening
💡 Why is hardening needed? When you install an operating system or set up a server, it comes open by default — configured for ease of use, not security. Hardening is the process of securing it by reducing attack surfaces and controlling access.

🌐 7. Network Hardening

Network hardening is the process of controlling access BEFORE someone reaches the system. It's your first line of defence.

Think of it as the security at the entrance of your apartment building — the guard, the locked gate, the intercom system. Only authorised people get past this point. If they can't reach your apartment door, they definitely can't break in.

Network hardening answers three key questions:

  • 🔸 Who is allowed to connect?
  • 🔸 From where can they connect?
  • 🔸 On which port can they connect?

Figure 4: Components of Network Hardening Architecture — Firewall, IDPS, Core Infrastructure, and VPN Gateway

🔧 Components of Network Hardening

🛡️ Firewall

A firewall is the primary gatekeeper. It examines incoming and outgoing traffic and blocks what doesn't meet the rules.

  • ACLs (Access Control Lists): Rules that define what traffic is allowed or denied
  • Port blocking: Block specific ports (e.g., block port 23 Telnet, allow port 443 HTTPS)
  • Geo-blocking: Block traffic from specific countries or IP ranges

AWS equivalent: Security Groups and Network ACLs (NACLs) act as firewalls in AWS.

🚨 IDPS System (Intrusion Detection and Prevention System)

If the firewall is the gate, IDPS is the security camera and alarm system that watches for suspicious behaviour even from authorised users.

  • Real-time alerts: Immediately notifies admins of suspicious activity
  • Anomaly detection: Flags unusual patterns (e.g., a user downloading 10GB of data at 3am)

AWS equivalent: AWS GuardDuty provides intelligent threat detection for your AWS environment.

🏗️ Core Infrastructure

The physical and logical network devices that route traffic through your organisation.

  • Router: Directs traffic between networks
  • Switch: Connects devices within the same network
  • Wireless access point: Provides WiFi — needs strong encryption (WPA3)

These need hardening too — default passwords changed, firmware updated, unused ports disabled.

🔒 VPN Gateway

VPN (Virtual Private Network) creates an encrypted tunnel for remote access — like a secure underground passage into your building.

  • IPsec tunnels: Encrypts all traffic between the user and the network
  • MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication): Requires two or more forms of identity verification

AWS equivalent: AWS Site-to-Site VPN and AWS Client VPN for secure remote access.

✅ Practical AWS Example of Network Hardening:
In your Security Group, you configure:
• Port 80 — open from the internet (for web traffic)
• Port 22 (SSH) — open ONLY from your admin IP address
• Everything else — blocked by default

This means even if someone finds your server's IP, they can't SSH into it unless they're on your admin machine. That's network hardening in action!

🖥️ 8. System Hardening

System hardening is the process of controlling access AFTER someone reaches the system. Once a user or process has passed network security and is inside, what can they do? System hardening answers that question.

Continuing our apartment analogy: you've passed the building gate (network hardening). Now you're inside the apartment. System hardening is about what you can do inside — which rooms you can enter, which drawers you can open, which appliances you can use.

Figure 5: The 5 Types of System Hardening

🔧 What System Hardening Controls

System hardening focuses on the following layers of a system:

💻 Operating System (OS) Hardening

The OS is the foundation — securing it protects everything running on top of it.

  • Remove unnecessary software and services (attack surface reduction)
  • Apply all security patches and updates promptly
  • Configure strong password policies
  • Disable unnecessary user accounts
  • Enable audit logging to track all activities

Example: A fresh Linux server has many services running by default. OS hardening disables FTP, Telnet, and other unused services, keeping only what's needed.

👤 User Access Control

Who gets access to what inside the system?

  • Principle of Least Privilege — give users only what they need
  • Use IAM-style role-based access control
  • Separate admin accounts from regular user accounts
  • Regularly review and remove stale accounts
⚡ Key principle: Users should have the minimum permissions needed to do their job — no more, no less. This is called the Principle of Least Privilege.

🔧 Service Hardening

Which services are allowed to run on the system?

  • Disable services not required for the system's purpose
  • Use service accounts with minimal permissions
  • Regularly audit running services

Example: A web server doesn't need to run a database service or FTP service. Disabling these reduces the attack surface.

📁 File and Permission Management

Who can read, write, or execute which files?

  • Set proper file permissions (e.g., sensitive config files readable only by root)
  • Encrypt sensitive files at rest
  • Regular permission audits
  • Integrity monitoring to detect unexpected file changes

🗄️ Database Hardening

Databases store your most valuable data — they need extra protection.

  • Remove default database users and sample databases
  • Encrypt data at rest and in transit
  • Restrict database access to only the application servers that need it
  • Implement query monitoring to detect unusual access patterns

AWS equivalent: RDS encryption, IAM database authentication, security group rules restricting DB access.

🖥️ Server Hardening

Physical and virtual server-level security measures.

  • Keep OS and software updated with security patches
  • Remove unnecessary software (bloatware reduction)
  • Configure host-based firewalls
  • Enable intrusion detection at the host level

AWS equivalent: AWS Systems Manager Patch Manager keeps your EC2 instances patched automatically.

❌ Common Mistake: People think setting up a server once is enough. Hardening is an ongoing process — new vulnerabilities are discovered regularly, and systems must be continuously monitored and updated.

📝 Overall Summary

  • WAF (Well-Architected Framework): A set of best practices for technical correctness of AWS workloads, applied AFTER migration. Has 6 pillars: Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Cost Optimization, Sustainability.
  • WAF is NOT a service: It's a framework (a checklist/guide) to evaluate and improve your architecture.
  • CAF (Cloud Adoption Framework): Helps organisations prepare for cloud migration — focused on organisational readiness. Has 6 perspectives: Business, People, Governance, Platform, Operations, Security.
  • WAF vs CAF: WAF = technical correctness AFTER migration; CAF = organisational readiness BEFORE/DURING migration.
  • Cloud is organisational change: Moving to cloud is not just infrastructure change — it requires people, process, and technology alignment (this is what CAF emphasises).
  • Security = controlling access: The core principle behind both system and network hardening.
  • Network Hardening: Controls access BEFORE reaching the system. Uses firewalls, IDPS, VPN gateways. In AWS: Security Groups, NACLs, GuardDuty.
  • System Hardening: Controls access AFTER reaching the system. Covers OS, users, services, files, databases. In AWS: IAM, Systems Manager, encryption.
  • Hardening is ongoing: Not a one-time activity — new vulnerabilities emerge constantly, requiring continuous monitoring and updates.
  • Principle of Least Privilege: A key concept in system hardening — give users minimum necessary permissions.

💼 Hands-On Projects

📋 Prerequisites

  • ✅ AWS account (Free Tier)
  • ✅ Knowledge: EC2, Security Groups, IAM (covered in previous sessions)

Project 1: Apply Network Hardening to an EC2 Instance

🎯 Objective: Configure Security Groups to allow only specific ports and sources — practical network hardening on AWS.

⏱️ Time: 20-30 min

💰 Cost: Free Tier

  1. Launch a new EC2 instance (Amazon Linux 2, t2.micro)
  2. During setup, create a new Security Group named "hardened-sg"
  3. Add inbound rule: HTTP (port 80) — Source: Anywhere (0.0.0.0/0)
  4. Add inbound rule: SSH (port 22) — Source: My IP only
  5. Do NOT add any other inbound rules
  6. Launch the instance and verify you can SSH from your machine
  7. Try SSH from a mobile hotspot or VPN (should fail — good!)
  8. Verification: Use online port scanners to check only port 80 is visible externally
  9. Cleanup: Terminate the instance to avoid charges

Project 2: WAF Self-Review with AWS Well-Architected Tool

🎯 Objective: Use the free AWS Well-Architected Tool to assess a workload against the 6 pillars.

⏱️ Time: 30-45 min

💰 Cost: Free

  1. Go to AWS Console → Search "Well-Architected Tool"
  2. Click "Define workload" → Name it "My Test Workload"
  3. Select Industry: Technology, Environment: Pre-production
  4. Start a review — answer questions across all 6 pillars
  5. Review the improvement plan it generates
  6. Note which pillar has the most "High Risk" items
  7. Cleanup: Delete the workload from the tool (no charges involved)

🤔 Reflection Questions

1. Your company just finished migrating 50 applications to AWS. A colleague says "We need to apply the CAF now." Is your colleague correct? Why or why not?

💡 Hint

Think about when CAF is used versus when WAF is used. The migration is already done — which framework applies now?

2. You open port 22 (SSH) on a Security Group to allow access from 0.0.0.0/0 (everywhere). Which hardening principle does this violate, and how would you fix it?

💡 Hint

Network hardening principle: control WHO can connect, FROM WHERE. SSH should only be accessible from trusted IP addresses.

3. A developer has root access to the production database "just in case they need it." What hardening principle does this violate?

💡 Hint

System hardening and IAM principle: what's the minimum access needed for their role?

4. Which WAF pillar is most relevant when your application crashes under high traffic during a sale event? What AWS services would address this?

💡 Hint

The app crashed when expected to work — think about which pillar ensures systems work correctly and consistently.

5. Your company's monthly AWS bill jumped 300% unexpectedly. Which WAF pillar should you focus on, and which AWS tool would help you investigate?

💡 Hint

Unexpected bills relate to one pillar specifically. AWS has a dedicated tool for cost analysis and visibility.

6. A new HR manager joins your company and needs help understanding why the People perspective of CAF matters. How would you explain it to them?

💡 Hint

Think about what happens when technology changes but employees aren't trained or prepared. How does that affect the migration?
