🌟 Introduction

Imagine you need a car. You could buy one outright, lease it for a year, rent it by the hour, or even bid for a car that someone else isn't using right now. Each option has a different price and works best for a different situation. AWS EC2 pricing works exactly the same way.

EC2 is AWS's virtual server service — it lets you rent computing power in the cloud. But instead of one fixed price, AWS offers five pricing models, each designed for a specific usage pattern. Pick the right model and you could save up to 90% compared to paying the highest rate. Pick the wrong one and you're throwing money away every month.

Figure 1: The five EC2 pricing models at a glance

In this session we covered all five models in detail — what they are, when to use them, how much they cost, and what makes each one unique. This is a high-frequency exam topic and an essential skill for any cloud role, because choosing the right pricing model is one of the first decisions you make when deploying a real-world application.

⚡ Why this matters for your career: Every company using AWS wants to reduce its cloud bill. If you can walk into an interview and explain how to cut EC2 costs by 60–90%, you immediately stand out from other candidates.

What You Will Learn

  • The five EC2 pricing models and the exact scenarios each is designed for
  • How to calculate savings compared to On-Demand pricing
  • The risks and trade-offs of each model
  • How vertical and horizontal scaling affect your cost
  • Exam traps and common interview questions on EC2 pricing

💡 Why EC2 Has Multiple Pricing Models

Before we dive into the models, it helps to understand why AWS created five different ways to pay for the same virtual server.

Different workloads behave very differently. A web application might run 24/7 for years. A batch job might only run on weekends. A machine learning experiment might run for 4 hours and never again. A bank has regulatory rules that prevent it from sharing hardware with anyone else. A startup needs to launch fast without any upfront commitment.

One pricing model cannot serve all these needs efficiently. So AWS designed five models — each optimised for a different usage pattern. The core principle is simple: the more you commit (time or capacity), the more you save.

Figure 2: Commitment level vs savings percentage across all five models

💡 The Golden Rule of EC2 Pricing: On-Demand is always the most expensive. Every other model exists to give you a discount in exchange for either a commitment, a risk, or a restriction.

🟠 Model 1: On-Demand Instances

On-Demand is the default pricing model — the one you get automatically if you launch an EC2 instance without choosing anything special. Think of it like a taxi: you get in, you pay per minute or per hour, you get out. No contracts, no commitments, no penalties.

Figure 3: On-Demand billing — pay only while the instance runs

How It Works

With On-Demand instances, you pay for compute capacity by the second (Linux/macOS) or by the hour (Windows), with no long-term commitments. You can start and stop instances at any time, and you only pay for what you actually use.

When to Use On-Demand

  • Unpredictable workloads — Applications whose traffic patterns you cannot forecast in advance
  • Short-term projects — Something you only need for a few days, weeks, or months
  • Development and testing — Dev environments that don't run 24/7
  • First time on AWS — When you're learning and haven't yet understood your usage patterns
  • Spiky traffic — Applications that go from near-zero to very high traffic unpredictably

Pricing Details

  • No upfront payment required
  • No long-term commitment
  • Pay only for what you use
  • The free tier gives you 750 hours per month of t2.micro or t3.micro On-Demand instances for the first 12 months.

❌ Common Mistake: Leaving On-Demand instances running 24/7 for predictable, steady-state workloads like production databases or web servers. This is the most expensive way to run a long-term workload. Use Reserved Instances or Savings Plans instead.

Impact of Scaling on On-Demand Cost

This connects directly to the question asked in class about vertical vs horizontal scaling and pricing:

  • Vertical scaling (scale up): You resize the instance to a larger type (e.g., t3.micro → t3.large). The hourly rate increases because you're paying for more vCPUs and RAM. Billed at the new rate from the moment the resize completes.
  • Horizontal scaling (scale out): You add more instances of the same type. Each new instance is billed independently at the On-Demand rate. Total cost = number of instances × hourly rate.

💡 In most real-world architectures, horizontal scaling is preferred because it's more fault-tolerant and often cheaper at scale — you can mix On-Demand with Spot for the extra capacity rather than paying the higher rate for a larger instance.

🔵 Model 2: Reserved Instances (RI)

Reserved Instances are not actually a separate type of server — they're a billing discount applied to On-Demand instances when you make a commitment to use a specific instance type in a specific region for 1 or 3 years.

Think of it like a gym membership. You're not getting a different gym — you're getting a lower per-visit price in exchange for signing a contract. The gym wins because they know you'll be there. AWS wins because it can plan its capacity. You win because you pay less.

Figure 4: Reserved Instances — same server, lower price through commitment

Savings

Reserved Instances provide up to 72% discount compared to On-Demand pricing.

Term Options

TermDiscountBest For
1-year~30–40%Moderate commitment, flexibility
3-year~60–72%Maximum savings, long-term workloads

Payment Options

Reserved Instances offer three payment options: All Upfront, Partial Upfront, and No Upfront.

  • All Upfront: Pay the full amount at the start → maximum discount
  • Partial Upfront: Pay a portion upfront + monthly charges → medium discount
  • No Upfront: Pay monthly with no initial cost → smallest discount, but no large initial spend

💡 Rule of thumb: If you can afford All Upfront, do it — you get the best rate. If you're a startup with limited cash, No Upfront still saves you 30–40% vs On-Demand over a year.

Types of Reserved Instances

There are three types of RIs, and this is an important exam area:

TypeFlexibilityDiscount
Standard RIFixed instance family, OS, regionHighest (~72%)
Convertible RICan change instance family, OS, tenancyLower (~54%)
Scheduled RIReserve capacity for specific time windowsModerate

⚡ Standard RIs cannot be exchanged for a different instance type. Convertible RIs can be exchanged but offer a lower discount.

Regional vs Zonal Scope

  • Regional RI: Discount applies to any instance in the region regardless of Availability Zone. Also provides a capacity reservation discount (but not a guaranteed capacity reservation).
  • Zonal RI: Discount applies only to the specified AZ, but provides a capacity reservation — AWS guarantees your instances will launch even during peak demand.

When to Use Reserved Instances

  • Production web servers running 24/7
  • Production databases (RDS, EC2-hosted)
  • Any steady-state workload you're confident will run for 1–3 years
  • Applications with predictable, consistent resource requirements

RI Marketplace

💡 According to AWS documentation, if you have Standard RIs you no longer need, you can sell them on the Reserved Instance Marketplace — a platform where AWS customers buy and sell unused reservations. This gives you an exit option if your needs change.
📚 Source: AWS Reserved Instance Marketplace Docs

🟡 Model 3: Spot Instances

Spot Instances are the most exciting and the most dangerous pricing model. Imagine AWS has a massive warehouse of servers. At any given moment, some of those servers are idle — nobody's using them. Rather than letting that capacity sit empty, AWS rents it out at a steep discount through a bidding mechanism. That's a Spot Instance.

The catch? When AWS needs that capacity back (because On-Demand or Reserved customers need it), your Spot Instance gets a 2-minute warning and then gets terminated. Your workload must be able to handle that interruption.

Figure 5: Spot Instances — deep discounts with interruption risk

Savings

Spot Instances can provide up to 90% discount compared to On-Demand pricing — the highest discount available across all EC2 pricing models.

How Spot Pricing Works

You set a maximum price you're willing to pay (your bid). AWS sets a Spot price that fluctuates based on supply and demand. As long as the Spot price stays below your bid, your instance keeps running. The moment the Spot price exceeds your bid, your instance gets the 2-minute warning and is terminated (or hibernated/stopped if configured).

Figure 6: Spot price fluctuations — your instance runs when price is below your bid

Handling Interruptions

The 2-minute warning is delivered via:

  • Instance metadata: Your application can poll http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/spot/termination-time
  • Amazon EventBridge: Automatically trigger a Lambda function or SNS notification
  • CloudWatch Events: Monitor for the EC2 Spot Instance Interruption Warning event

When to Use Spot Instances

  • Batch processing — Data pipelines, ETL jobs, image/video processing
  • Machine learning training — Long training runs that can checkpoint and restart
  • Big data analytics — Hadoop, Spark clusters
  • Rendering farms — 3D rendering, scientific simulations
  • CI/CD pipelines — Build and test environments
  • Stateless web servers — As part of a mixed fleet with On-Demand for minimum capacity

When NOT to Use Spot Instances

❌ Never use Spot Instances for:

  • Production databases (interruption = data loss or corruption)
  • Critical stateful applications that cannot tolerate interruption
  • Real-time customer-facing workloads where a 2-minute termination would break the user experience

Spot Fleet

A Spot Fleet is a collection of Spot Instances (and optionally On-Demand instances) that AWS manages for you. You specify your target capacity and AWS fulfils it by mixing instance types and Availability Zones to minimise interruption risk. This is the recommended way to run Spot workloads in production.

💡 Pro Tip: Use Spot Fleet with Diversified allocation strategy — spread your instances across multiple instance types and AZs. This dramatically reduces the chance of all your instances being interrupted at once.

🟢 Model 4: Savings Plans

Savings Plans are the newest and most flexible way to save on AWS compute. Instead of committing to a specific instance type (like Reserved Instances), you commit to a minimum spend per hour for 1 or 3 years. In exchange, AWS gives you a discount on all compute usage that falls under your committed amount.

Think of it like a phone plan: you pay for X minutes per month, and within that bucket, you can use any combination of calls and data. Savings Plans let you commit to a dollar amount, and within that amount, you can mix and match EC2 types, regions, and even other services.

Figure 7: Savings Plans vs Reserved Instances — same savings, more flexibility

Types of Savings Plans

There are three types, and each has different scope:

⚡ Compute Savings Plans are the most flexible — they automatically apply to EC2, Fargate, and Lambda usage regardless of instance family, size, region, or operating system.

When to Use Savings Plans

  • You know you'll spend a certain amount on compute every month but your instance mix changes frequently
  • You're using a combination of EC2, Fargate, and Lambda
  • You want Reserved Instance-level savings without being locked to a specific instance type
  • You're scaling rapidly and your instance family needs may change

🔴 Model 5: Dedicated Hosts

A Dedicated Host is a physical server that AWS reserves exclusively for your use. No other AWS customer shares the underlying hardware with you. You get the entire physical machine — all its CPU sockets, cores, and memory — to yourself.

This sounds like overkill, but it's essential for two specific situations: compliance requirements and software licensing.

Figure 8: Shared tenancy vs Dedicated Host — same instance, different isolation level

Why Would You Need This?

  • Compliance and regulatory requirements: Industries like healthcare (HIPAA), finance (PCI-DSS), and government often require proof that workloads don't share hardware with other companies. A Dedicated Host gives you that isolation.
  • Bring Your Own License (BYOL): Some software vendors (like Microsoft, Oracle, SAP) license their software per physical socket or core — not per virtual machine. On shared infrastructure you can't count physical sockets. On a Dedicated Host, you can use your existing licenses.

Pricing

Dedicated Hosts are the most expensive EC2 pricing model because you're paying for an entire physical server, not just a virtual instance. You can purchase Dedicated Hosts On-Demand (by the hour) or as a 1 or 3-year reservation for a discount.

Dedicated Hosts vs Dedicated Instances

This is a very common exam trap. They sound similar but are different:

FeatureDedicated InstanceDedicated Host
Hardware isolationYour VMs don't share hardware with other accountsYou have an entire physical server
Visibility into hardwareNo — you can't see socket/core detailsYes — full visibility into sockets, cores, host ID
BYOL supportNoYes
Control instance placementNoYes
CostLowerHigher

❌ Exam Trap: "Dedicated Instance" and "Dedicated Host" are NOT the same. Dedicated Instance means no hardware sharing with other AWS accounts. Dedicated Host means you own the entire physical machine and can see socket/core details for licensing purposes.

When to Use Dedicated Hosts

  • Regulated industries requiring hardware isolation (healthcare, finance, government)
  • Workloads with BYOL software (Microsoft Windows Server, Microsoft SQL Server, Oracle Database)
  • Compliance audits that require detailed hardware reports

💹 How Scaling Affects Your Pricing

A question was raised in class about whether vertical or horizontal scaling makes a difference in pricing. The answer depends on which pricing model you're using.

Figure 10: Vertical vs horizontal scaling impact on EC2 billing

Vertical Scaling (Scale Up)

  • You change to a larger instance type (e.g., t3.micro → t3.xlarge)
  • Billing rate changes to the new instance type's rate immediately
  • If you have a Reserved Instance, resizing a Reserved Instance to a different family (e.g., t3 → c5) requires a Convertible RI — Standard RIs cannot change instance family.
  • With Savings Plans: the discount automatically adjusts to the new instance — no action needed

Horizontal Scaling (Scale Out)

  • You add more instances of the same type (e.g., 2 × t3.large → 6 × t3.large)
  • Each additional instance is billed at its own rate
  • Smart cost strategy: Keep a baseline of Reserved Instances for minimum capacity, use On-Demand for moderate spikes, and Spot for burst capacity — this is called a mixed fleet strategy
  • With Auto Scaling, you can automate the spin-up of Spot Instances during scale-out events to minimise cost

✅ Best Practice (Mixed Fleet): Use Reserved Instances for your guaranteed minimum load (say 3 instances always running), On-Demand for predictable growth (2–3 more during business hours), and Spot for unpredictable spikes. This gives you cost efficiency at every tier.

🎓 Exam Tips & Common Traps

High-Frequency Exam Questions

  • Q: Which pricing model provides the highest discount?
    A: Spot Instances — up to 90% off On-Demand
  • Q: Which model guarantees capacity even during peak demand?
    A: Zonal Reserved Instances — they provide a capacity reservation in the specific AZ
  • Q: A company has a compliance requirement to not share hardware with other customers. What should they use?
    A: Dedicated Hosts (not Dedicated Instances if they also need BYOL)
  • Q: A data scientist runs batch ML training jobs that can restart if interrupted. What's the most cost-effective option?
    A: Spot Instances
  • Q: What happens to a Spot Instance when the Spot price exceeds your bid?
    A: You receive a 2-minute interruption notice, then the instance is terminated

Common Exam Traps

❌ Trap 1: "Dedicated Instance" = "Dedicated Host" — They are NOT the same. Dedicated Instance = no hardware sharing. Dedicated Host = whole physical machine + socket/core visibility + BYOL support.

❌ Trap 2: Standard RI can be exchanged for different instance types — FALSE. Only Convertible RIs can be exchanged.

❌ Trap 3: Savings Plans and Reserved Instances are the same thing — FALSE. Savings Plans commit to a spend per hour (flexible). RIs commit to a specific instance configuration.

❌ Trap 4: Spot Instances are billed by the hour — FALSE. Like On-Demand Linux instances, Spot is billed per second (minimum 1 minute).

📝 Overall Summary

  • On-Demand: Pay per second/hour with no commitment — most expensive, most flexible; best for unpredictable or short-term workloads
  • Reserved Instances: Up to 72% discount in exchange for a 1 or 3-year commitment to a specific instance type — best for steady, predictable long-term workloads
  • Spot Instances: Up to 90% discount on unused AWS capacity — cheapest option, but comes with interruption risk and a 2-minute termination warning; best for fault-tolerant batch workloads
  • Savings Plans: Up to 66–72% discount in exchange for committing to a minimum spend per hour — more flexible than RIs, applies to EC2, Fargate, and Lambda (Compute SP)
  • Dedicated Hosts: Entire physical server for your exclusive use — most expensive, required for compliance and BYOL software licensing
  • Key exam number: Spot = up to 90% off, Reserved = up to 72% off, Savings Plans = up to 66–72% off
  • Vertical scaling increases the instance size (and its billing rate); horizontal scaling adds more instances (each billed independently)
  • Mixed fleet strategy combines Reserved (baseline) + On-Demand (moderate spikes) + Spot (burst) for maximum cost efficiency
  • Standard RIs cannot change instance family; Convertible RIs can, but at a lower discount
  • Dedicated Instance ≠ Dedicated Host — a critical distinction for both exams and real-world decisions

Project 4: Launch, Stop, and Start an EC2 Instance — Track the Billing Impact

🎯 Objective: Launch a real On-Demand EC2 instance, stop it, restart it, and observe exactly how billing behaves at each stage. Understand the difference between a stopped instance and a terminated instance.

⏱️ Time: 30–45 min

💰 Cost: Free tier eligible — use t2.micro or t3.micro within your 750 free hours/month

💰 Cost Note: A running t2.micro costs approximately $0.0116/hr outside the free tier. Always terminate (not just stop) when done if you are near or past your free tier limit. A stopped instance does NOT incur compute charges but DOES incur EBS storage charges (~$0.10/GB/month).

  1. Log in to the AWS Console and navigate to EC2 → Instances → Launch Instances.

    📸 Screenshot: EC2 Instances page — click 'Launch instances'

  2. Configure the instance:

    • Instance type: t2.micro or t3.micro (free tier)
    • Key pair: Create a new key pair named pricing-test-key (download and save the .pem file)
    • Network settings: Allow SSH traffic from My IP
    • Storage: 8 GiB gp3 (default)

    📸 Screenshot: Launch wizard — name, AMI, instance type, and key pair configured

  3. Click Launch instance. AWS will confirm the instance is launching. Click View all instances.

    📸 Screenshot: Launch success confirmation — note your instance ID

  4. Wait until the instance Instance State shows Running and the Status checks column shows 2/2 checks passed. This confirms the instance is healthy and billing is active.

    📸 Screenshot: Instance running — billing is active from this moment

  5. Now stop the instance: select the checkbox next to your instance → Instance State → Stop instance → Stop.

    📸 Screenshot: Stop instance — Instance state → Stop instance

  6. Wait for the state to change to Stopped. Observe that the instance still appears in the list — it still exists and still has its EBS volume attached (which incurs storage charges). Compute charges have stopped.

Project 6: Spot Instance Interruption Simulation — Design a Recovery Plan

🎯 Objective: Launch a real Spot Instance, manually simulate an interruption by stopping it, then design a recovery architecture. This project builds the engineering maturity to use Spot safely in production.

⏱️ Time: 45–60 min

💰 Cost: Minimal — Spot t3.micro costs approximately $0.003–$0.005/hr. The instance will run for 10–15 minutes maximum.

💰 Cost Note: Spot Instances are billed per second (minimum 1 minute). A t3.micro Spot Instance running for 15 minutes costs less than $0.002. Terminate immediately after the project to avoid further charges.

  1. Navigate to EC2 → Instances → Launch Instances. Name it spot-test-instance.

  2. Choose Amazon Linux 2023 AMI and instance type t3.micro.

  3. Expand Advanced details at the bottom of the launch wizard. Find the Purchasing option field and select Request Spot Instances. Leave the maximum price as the default (On-Demand price).

    📸 Screenshot: Advanced details → Request Spot Instances enabled

  4. Use an existing key pair or create a new one. Under Network settings, allow SSH from your IP. Click Launch instance.

  5. Navigate to EC2 → Spot Requests in the left sidebar. You will see your Spot request with status fulfilled once the instance is running.

    📸 Screenshot: Spot Requests page — status 'fulfilled' means your instance is running

  6. Go back to EC2 → Instances. Find spot-test-instance — it is running as a Spot Instance. Note the Lifecycle column shows spot.

    📸 Screenshot: Instances list — Lifecycle column shows 'spot' for your instance

  7. Simulate the interruption: Select the instance → Instance State → Stop instance. This mimics what AWS does when the Spot price exceeds your bid. Confirm and wait for it to reach Stopped state.

    📸 Screenshot: Spot instance stopped — simulating an interruption event

✅ Model Recovery Architecture: Batch job writes checkpoint to S3 every 30 minutes. An EventBridge rule detects the EC2 Spot interruption warning event and triggers a Lambda function that (1) writes a final checkpoint, (2) launches an On-Demand fallback instance, and (3) sends an SNS alert to the team. On-Demand instance reads the S3 checkpoint and resumes the job. Maximum data loss: 30 minutes of work.

🤔 Reflection Questions

1. A startup is launching a new SaaS product. They have no historical data on traffic patterns. Which EC2 pricing model should they use at launch, and when should they consider switching?

💡 Hint

Think about what you know vs what you don't at launch. What model requires zero commitment? After 3–6 months of traffic data, what pattern might emerge that would justify a commitment?

2. A bank's risk department says all production workloads must run on hardware that no other company has ever used, and they need to use their existing Oracle Database licenses. Which pricing model fits, and why can't they use Dedicated Instances instead?

💡 Hint

Focus on two requirements: hardware isolation AND license portability. Which model provides both? What's the key difference between Dedicated Instances and Dedicated Hosts regarding license tracking?

3. A data engineering team runs nightly ETL jobs from 2 AM to 6 AM that process 10 TB of data. The jobs use checkpointing so they can restart from where they left off. What is the most cost-effective EC2 pricing model, and what risk must the team manage?

💡 Hint

The job is fault-tolerant (it can restart). It runs at a specific time window. Which model gives the biggest discount? What risk comes with it, and how does the checkpointing feature help?

4. Your company currently uses Standard Reserved Instances for a fleet of m5.xlarge instances. The engineering team wants to migrate to c5.xlarge instances (a different family) for better CPU performance. What problem do you face, and what are your options?

💡 Hint

Standard RIs are locked to an instance family. What can you do with an RI you no longer need? What type of RI would have given you the flexibility to change families?

5. Explain the mixed fleet strategy to a non-technical manager in 3 sentences. Why does it save money compared to using only On-Demand or only Reserved Instances?

💡 Hint

Think about base load vs peak load. Reserved for guaranteed minimum, On-Demand for moderate growth, Spot for the unpredictable bursts. What would happen to costs if you used only On-Demand for the same scenario?
