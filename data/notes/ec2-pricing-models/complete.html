📘 Introduction

Imagine you're renting office space. You could rent by the hour at full price (perfect for a short project), sign a one-year lease for a discount (great if you know you'll be there long-term), or grab last-minute empty desks at a huge markdown (risky, but cheap). AWS EC2 pricing works the same way — and knowing which model to pick is one of the most valuable skills you can have as a cloud professional.

In the previous sessions, we covered On-Demand and Reserved Instances — the two most straightforward pricing models. Today we complete the picture with three more powerful options: Savings Plans, Spot Instances, and Dedicated Hosts. Each solves a different business problem, and in real-world interviews and on the AWS exam, you will almost certainly be asked which one to recommend.

Figure 1: EC2 Pricing Models — Five Ways to Pay for Compute

⚡ Why This Matters in Real Life: Pricing decisions directly affect company costs. A poorly chosen pricing model on a large deployment can mean thousands of dollars in unnecessary monthly charges. This is why every technical interview asks: "Which EC2 pricing model would you recommend for this scenario?"

What We Already Covered

Before diving in, here's a quick recap of what was covered in previous sessions so today's content makes full sense:

  • On-Demand: Pay by the hour/second with no commitment. Most expensive per hour. Best for unpredictable workloads or short experiments.
  • Reserved Instances (RI): Commit to a specific instance type, region, and OS for 1 or 3 years. Up to 72% discount vs On-Demand. Best for steady, predictable workloads.

Today we add Savings Plans (a flexible RI alternative), Spot Instances (spare capacity at huge discounts), and Dedicated Hosts (your own physical server).

💰 Savings Plans

Think of a Savings Plans like a gym membership. You tell the gym: "I commit to spending $50 a month for the next year." In return, they give you a discounted rate on everything. You're not locking yourself to a specific class at a specific time — just committing to a spending level. AWS Savings Plans work exactly the same way.

A Savings Plan is a flexible pricing model where you commit to a specific dollar amount of compute usage per hour for 1 or 3 years, in exchange for a discount of up to 72% compared to On-Demand pricing.

Figure 2: How Savings Plans Work — Commit to Spend, Not to a Specific Instance

Savings Plan vs Reserved Instance — What's the Difference?

This is a very common exam question. Both give you a discount for committing, but the key difference is flexibility:

FeatureReserved InstanceSavings Plan
What you commit toSpecific instance type, region, OSDollar/hour spend amount
FlexibilityLow — locked to configurationHigh — applies across instance types
Services coveredEC2 onlyEC2, Fargate, Lambda (Compute SP)
Max discountUp to 72%Up to 66–72%
Term options1 or 3 years1 or 3 years

💡 Key Insight: Savings Plans are newer and more flexible than Reserved Instances. AWS recommends Savings Plans for most new customers. Reserved Instances are still useful when you need instance-type-specific discounts (like when using a very specific instance family).

Types of Savings Plans

There are three types of Savings Plans, and the exam loves asking about the difference:

Figure 3: Three Types of AWS Savings Plans

1. Compute Savings Plan — Most flexible. Applies to any EC2 instance (any region, any family, any size, any OS), plus AWS Fargate and AWS Lambda. Compute Savings Plans offer up to 66% discount and automatically apply as your usage changes — you can change instance families, sizes, OS, or even move between EC2, Fargate, and Lambda.

2. EC2 Instance Savings Plan — Less flexible but deeper discount. You commit to a specific instance family in a specific region (e.g., T3 in us-east-1), but you can still change sizes, OS, and tenancy within that family. EC2 Instance Savings Plans offer up to 72% discount — the deepest available through Savings Plans.

3. SageMaker Savings Plan — Only for Amazon SageMaker ML workloads. Not relevant for general EC2 usage.

How to Calculate Your Per-Hour Commitment

This is where people get confused. Let's walk through it step-by-step using the example from class.

Imagine you're currently running 5 instances of T3.medium. Here's how to calculate your hourly commitment:

  1. Go to EC2 Console → Savings Plans → Recommendations — AWS will calculate this for you automatically based on your current usage.
  2. Alternatively, use the AWS Pricing Calculator at calculator.aws to compare On-Demand vs Savings Plan costs.
  3. Take your current monthly spend and divide by 730 (average hours per month) to get your per-hour baseline.
  4. Decide what percentage of that usage you want to commit. Most companies commit 70–80% of baseline, keeping 20–30% on On-Demand for bursts.

Figure 4: How to Calculate Your Savings Plan Per-Hour Commitment

In class, the example was: if you're running T3.medium at $0.041/hour and committing to 720 hours (one month), and the total comes to $150/month — AWS will discount this by approximately 30% for a 1-year plan, bringing your monthly cost down to around $105.

Payment Options

Savings Plans offer three payment options: All Upfront (highest discount, pay everything at start), Partial Upfront (some upfront, rest monthly), and No Upfront (lowest discount, pay monthly).

All three payment options are available for both 1-year and 3-year terms. Paying all upfront gives you the biggest discount because AWS has certainty about your payment.

How to Purchase a Savings Plan (Step-by-Step)

Here's how you purchase a Savings Plan from the AWS Console, as demonstrated in class:

  1. Sign in to the AWS Management Console
  2. Navigate to EC2 → in the left sidebar, find Savings Plans → click Purchase Savings Plans
  3. Choose the Savings Plan type: Compute, EC2 Instance, or SageMaker
  4. Select the Term: 1 year or 3 years
  5. Select the Region (e.g., us-east-1 North Virginia) and Instance Family (e.g., T3)
  6. Enter your Per-hour commitment amount (e.g., $0.041)
  7. Choose your Payment option: All Upfront, Partial Upfront, or No Upfront
  8. Review the estimated monthly cost and annual cost shown on screen
  9. Click Add to Cart → Confirm and Purchase

📚 Pro Tip: Before purchasing, always check the Recommendations tab in the Savings Plans console. AWS analyzes your last 7, 30, or 60 days of usage and recommends the optimal commitment amount. This prevents over-committing (paying for capacity you won't use) or under-committing (missing out on full discounts).

📚 Source: AWS Savings Plans Documentation

✅ Best Practice: Use the AWS Cost Explorer Savings Plans recommendations before purchasing. It shows you the exact savings you'd get and the break-even point. Never guess your commitment amount — always base it on actual usage data.

⚡ Spot Instances

Here's a great analogy: imagine an airline that has empty seats on a flight that's about to depart. They could either fly with empty seats (wasted capacity) or sell those seats at a steep discount to last-minute travelers. The catch? If a full-price customer wants the seat, you get bumped. AWS Spot Instances work exactly like this — you're using AWS's spare EC2 capacity at a massive discount, but AWS can reclaim those instances when they need the capacity back.

Spot Instances allow you to use spare EC2 capacity at up to 90% discount compared to On-Demand prices, but AWS can terminate your instance with a 2-minute warning when the capacity is needed.

Figure 5: Spot Instance Lifecycle — From Request to Termination

How Spot Instances Work

Here's what happens behind the scenes when you request a Spot Instance:

  1. You submit a Spot Instance Request specifying the instance type, AMI, and your maximum price you're willing to pay (or you accept the current Spot price).
  2. AWS checks its spare capacity pool. If capacity is available and the Spot price is at or below your max price, AWS launches your instance.
  3. Your instance runs as long as: (a) spare capacity exists, and (b) the current Spot price stays at or below your max price.
  4. If AWS needs the capacity back (because On-Demand or Reserved Instance customers need it), AWS sends an interruption notice 2 minutes before termination.
  5. Your instance is terminated (or stopped/hibernated, depending on your interruption behavior setting).

💡 What happens to your data? By default, the root EBS volume is deleted when a Spot Instance is terminated. If you need to preserve data, use a separate EBS volume (not the root) or enable Stop behavior instead of Terminate in your Spot request settings.

📚 Source: AWS EC2 Spot Instance Interruptions Docs

Spot Pricing Model

Spot pricing is dynamic — the Spot price changes based on supply and demand for each instance type in each Availability Zone. AWS publishes the current Spot price, and it fluctuates throughout the day. You can view the Spot Price History in the EC2 console to understand typical price ranges for an instance type.

Figure 6: Spot Prices Differ by Availability Zone — Always Check Multiple AZs

When to Use Spot Instances

Spot Instances are only suitable for workloads that can handle unexpected interruptions. The golden question to ask is: "If this instance disappears right now with 2 minutes notice, is that okay?"

✅ GOOD use cases for Spot Instances:

  • Batch processing — rendering jobs, data transformation, log analysis (can be checkpointed and resumed)
  • Big Data analytics — Hadoop/Spark jobs, EMR clusters (stateless workers)
  • CI/CD pipelines — running automated tests (can be re-triggered if interrupted)
  • Machine Learning training — training jobs can use checkpoints to resume after interruption
  • Stateless web application workers — processing queued tasks where each task is independent
  • Image/video processing — encoding pipelines where tasks are small and restartable

❌ BAD use cases for Spot Instances:

  • Production databases — data loss risk if interrupted without proper snapshots
  • Critical web servers — if your main server gets terminated, users see downtime
  • Long-running stateful jobs without checkpointing
  • Jobs that can't be interrupted mid-way without losing all progress

Spot Fleet — Managing Multiple Spot Instances

A Spot Fleet is a collection of Spot Instances (and optionally On-Demand Instances) that is managed together to meet a target capacity while minimizing cost.

Instead of requesting a single instance type in a single AZ (risky — low availability), a Spot Fleet lets you specify multiple instance types and multiple AZs. AWS automatically diversifies across your pool to get capacity. If one instance type gets expensive or unavailable, AWS shifts to another.

Figure 7: Spot Fleet Architecture — Diversify Across Types and AZs for Resilience

Handling Spot Interruptions Gracefully

The key to using Spot Instances successfully is designing for interruption from day one. Here are the three interruption behaviors you can configure:

  • Terminate — Default. Instance is deleted when interrupted. Root volume deleted (unless configured otherwise).
  • Stop — Instance is stopped (not terminated). Root volume preserved. You can restart it later when capacity is available. Works only with persistent Spot requests.
  • Hibernate — Instance RAM is written to the EBS root volume, then the instance stops. On restart, RAM is reloaded — your applications resume exactly where they left off. Requires an encrypted root EBS volume.

⚡ Exam Tip: If a question asks "how to resume an application exactly where it left off after a Spot interruption" → the answer is Hibernate. If the question just asks about preserving storage → Stop is sufficient. If availability doesn't matter → Terminate (default) is fine.

🏢 Dedicated Hosts

Most of the time, when you launch an EC2 instance, your virtual machine shares a physical server with other AWS customers (though completely isolated and secure). That's the default — and it's fine for 99% of workloads. But sometimes, businesses have strict requirements that say: "Our software must run on a physical server that only WE use." That's exactly what a Dedicated Host solves.

A Dedicated Host is a physical EC2 server that is fully dedicated to your use — no other AWS customer shares the underlying hardware.

Figure 8: Shared Tenancy vs Dedicated Host — One Physical Server, Your Use Only

Why Would You Need a Dedicated Host?

There are three main business reasons for choosing a Dedicated Host, and the exam loves testing all three:

1. Software Licensing (BYOL — Bring Your Own License)

Certain enterprise software licenses (like Windows Server, Oracle Database, SQL Server) are tied to physical cores or physical sockets — not virtual CPUs. These licenses require you to know exactly how many physical cores and sockets the server has.

On a shared EC2 instance, AWS doesn't expose the underlying physical topology. A Dedicated Host does — you can see the number of physical cores and sockets, which lets you use your existing per-core or per-socket licenses (BYOL). This can save enormous licensing costs for large Oracle or Windows deployments.

2. Compliance and Regulatory Requirements

Some regulated industries (healthcare, finance, government) have policies requiring that workloads run on hardware not shared with other organizations. Dedicated Hosts provide this physical isolation guarantee with full documentation for auditors.

3. Visibility and Control Over Hardware Placement

With a Dedicated Host, you control instance placement on the physical server. This matters for licensing (to avoid exceeding per-host license limits) and for understanding the exact hardware your workloads run on.

Figure 9: Three Key Reasons to Use a Dedicated Host

Dedicated Host vs Dedicated Instance — A Common Confusion

These two sound similar but are different — the exam frequently tries to trick you here:

FeatureDedicated InstanceDedicated Host
What is it?Your instance runs on hardware dedicated to your accountYou rent an entire physical server
Physical server visibilityNo — you don't see physical topologyYes — cores, sockets visible
Instance placement controlNo controlFull control
BYOL supportLimitedFull support
Per-host billingNo — per instanceYes — billed per host
Use caseBasic isolation (compliance)BYOL, deep licensing control

⚡ Key Exam Difference: If the question mentions BYOL (Bring Your Own License) or needing to see physical core/socket count → always choose Dedicated Host, not Dedicated Instance.

Dedicated Host Pricing

Dedicated Hosts are priced per physical host per hour. The price depends on the instance family the host supports. You can purchase Dedicated Hosts as:

  • On-Demand — Pay hourly, no commitment. Most expensive.
  • Reserved — 1 or 3 year commitment, up to 70% discount vs On-Demand Dedicated Host pricing.

Note that the discount percentages for Dedicated Hosts are relative to Dedicated Host On-Demand pricing — not regular EC2 On-Demand pricing (Dedicated Hosts are more expensive per hour than shared instances to begin with).

According to AWS documentation, Dedicated Hosts can be shared with other accounts within the same AWS Organization using RAM (Resource Access Manager). This lets you centrally purchase a Dedicated Host and share its capacity with multiple teams or accounts.

📚 Source: AWS Dedicated Hosts Documentation

Scenario-Based Practice Questions

In interviews and on the exam, you'll be given a scenario and asked to recommend a pricing model. Let's practice:

Scenario 1: A company runs a web application that receives steady traffic throughout the year. They want maximum savings and are happy to commit for 3 years.
Answer: Reserved Instance (3-year, All Upfront) — maximum discount for predictable, steady workload.

Scenario 2: A startup runs nightly batch jobs to process log files. Jobs take 4 hours but can be restarted if they fail. They want the cheapest option.
Answer: Spot Instances — batch jobs are interruptible, and the 90% discount makes this perfect.

Scenario 3: A financial company needs to run Oracle Database with per-core licensing on EC2. Their compliance team requires physical server isolation.
Answer: Dedicated Host — BYOL with physical core visibility + compliance isolation.

Scenario 4: A company's development team needs servers for testing new features. Usage is irregular — sometimes 2 hours, sometimes 8 hours per day.
Answer: On-Demand — no commitment, flexible, ideal for unpredictable dev/test.

Scenario 5: A company currently uses Reserved Instances but now wants to move some workloads to AWS Lambda. They want a single discount mechanism covering both EC2 and Lambda.
Answer: Compute Savings Plan — it covers EC2, Lambda, and Fargate with a single commitment.

🎓 Exam Tips — Pricing Models

EC2 pricing models appear frequently in both the AWS Cloud Practitioner and AWS Solutions Architect Associate exams. Here are the key patterns to remember:

  • Spot Instances provide the highest discount (up to 90%) but can be interrupted with a 2-minute warning — only suitable for fault-tolerant workloads.
  • Savings Plans are more flexible than Reserved Instances — they apply to any instance family, size, OS, region (Compute SP), or even across services like Lambda and Fargate.
  • Dedicated Hosts are the only option that supports BYOL and gives you visibility into physical core/socket topology.
  • If a scenario asks for maximum savings with NO interruption risk, the answer is Reserved Instances (3-year, All Upfront) or EC2 Instance Savings Plan (3-year, All Upfront).
  • Always distinguish between Dedicated Instances (hardware isolated per account, no visibility) and Dedicated Hosts (full physical server, BYOL support, placement control).
  • The AWS Pricing Calculator is used to estimate and compare costs before purchasing — it is NOT a cost monitoring tool (that's AWS Cost Explorer or AWS Budgets).

📝 Overall Summary

  • Savings Plans: Commit to a $/hour spend for 1 or 3 years in exchange for up to 72% discount. Flexible — applies across instance types, sizes, OS, and regions. Three types: Compute (most flexible), EC2 Instance (deepest discount), SageMaker.
  • Savings Plan Payment Options: All Upfront (best discount), Partial Upfront, No Upfront — available for both 1-year and 3-year terms.
  • Spot Instances: Use AWS spare capacity at up to 90% discount. Can be interrupted with only 2-minute warning. Best for batch, CI/CD, ML training, and other fault-tolerant workloads.
  • Spot Interruption Behaviors: Terminate (default), Stop (preserves storage), Hibernate (saves RAM state — requires encrypted EBS).
  • Spot Fleet: Manages a pool of Spot (and optionally On-Demand) instances across multiple types and AZs for resilience.
  • Dedicated Hosts: Your own physical EC2 server — no sharing with other customers. Required for BYOL licenses (Oracle, Windows Server) tied to physical cores/sockets.
  • Dedicated Host vs Dedicated Instance: Dedicated Host = full physical server visibility + BYOL + placement control. Dedicated Instance = account-isolated hardware, no visibility.
  • Decision Framework: Unpredictable → On-Demand. Predictable + flexible → Savings Plans. Predictable + specific type → Reserved. Fault-tolerant + cheapest → Spot. BYOL/compliance → Dedicated Host.
  • AWS Pricing Calculator: Free tool at calculator.aws to compare pricing model costs before committing.
  • Key Numbers: Spot = up to 90% off. Savings Plans / Reserved = up to 72% off. Spot interruption warning = 2 minutes.

Project 5: Auto Scaling Group with Mixed On-Demand + Spot Capacity

🎯 Objective: Create an Auto Scaling Group that uses On-Demand instances for a guaranteed minimum capacity and Spot Instances to cheaply handle demand spikes — the same pattern used by companies to cut EC2 costs by 40–60% while maintaining availability.

⏱️ Time: 45-60 min

💰 Cost: Minimal — t3.micro On-Demand + Spot. Terminate the ASG and all instances at the end. Estimated under $0.05 for the session.

💰 Cost Note: Auto Scaling Groups can re-launch instances automatically if you don't delete them. After this project, delete the ASG first (which terminates instances), then delete the Launch Template. This prevents any unexpected re-launches.

  1. In the EC2 Console, navigate to Launch Templates in the left sidebar → click Create launch template. Fill in:

    • Template name: mixed-capacity-template
    • AMI: Amazon Linux 2
    • Instance type: t3.micro
    • Key pair: your existing key pair
    • Security group: allow SSH (port 22)

    Leave everything else as default. Click Create launch template.

    Figure 28: 📸 Create Launch Template — the reusable config for the ASG

  2. In the left sidebar, navigate to Auto Scaling Groups → Create Auto Scaling group. Name it mixed-pricing-asg. Select the launch template you just created. Click Next.

  3. On the Choose instance launch options step, select Override launch template to enable mixed instance policies. Under Instance distribution:

    • Set On-Demand base capacity to 1 — this guarantees at least 1 On-Demand instance always runs.

Project 6: Fault-Tolerant Spot System with Job Queue (Portfolio Level)

🎯 Objective: Build the solution to Spot's biggest problem — interrupted jobs. You'll create a Spot Instance that processes tasks from an SQS queue, checkpoint progress to S3, and demonstrate that when the instance is terminated, no work is lost because the next instance resumes from where the previous one stopped. This is a real production pattern used for ML training, video encoding, and data pipelines.

⏱️ Time: 60-90 min

💰 Cost: Under $0.10. SQS and S3 are free tier. t3.micro Spot is ~$0.003/hr.

💰 Cost Note: SQS charges per million requests (first million free). S3 charges for storage (first 5GB free). Terminate the Spot Instance and delete the SQS queue and S3 bucket contents after the project.

Part A — Set Up the Queue and Storage

  1. Go to SQS in the AWS Console → Create queue. Choose Standard Queue. Name it job-queue. Leave all defaults. Click Create queue.

    Figure 33: 📸 Create SQS queue — job-queue for task distribution

  2. Send 5 test messages to the queue. In the SQS console, click the job-queue → Send and receive messages → enter a message body like {"task_id": 1, "payload": "process-file-001.csv"}. Repeat for task_id 2 through 5.

    Figure 34: 📸 5 messages in the queue — each represents a job task

  3. Go to S3 → Create bucket. Name it spot-checkpoints-[your-account-id] (bucket names must be globally unique). Region = us-east-1. Leave all defaults. Click Create bucket. This bucket stores job progress checkpoints.

Part B — Launch and Run the Spot Worker

  1. Launch a Spot t3.micro instance (Amazon Linux 2). In Advanced Details, attach an IAM instance profile that has AmazonSQSFullAccess and AmazonS3FullAccess permissions (create a simple role with these policies if you don't have one). Enable Request Spot Instances.

    💡 Why the IAM role? The instance needs permissions to read from SQS and write checkpoints to S3. Attaching a role (instead of hardcoding credentials) is always the correct approach — this is also what AWS and security best practices require.

  2. SSH into the Spot instance and run this worker script:

    #!/bin/bash# Install AWS CLI if not presentsudo yum install -y aws-cliQUEUE_URL="https://sqs.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/YOUR_ACCOUNT_ID/job-queue"BUCKET="spot-checkpoints-YOUR_ACCOUNT_ID"echo "Spot worker started. Processing messages from queue..."while true; do  # Receive one message  MSG=$(aws sqs receive-message \    --queue-url $QUEUE_URL \    --max-number-of-messages 1 \    --region us-east-1)    BODY=$(echo $MSG | python3 -c "import sys,json; m=json.load(sys.stdin); print(m['Messages'][0]['Body'])" 2>/dev/null)  RECEIPT=$(echo $MSG | python3 -c "import sys,json; m=json.load(sys.stdin); print(m['Messages'][0]['ReceiptHandle'])" 2>/dev/null)    if [ -z "$BODY" ]; then    echo "Queue empty. Worker done."    break  fi    TASK_ID=$(echo $BODY | python3 -c "import sys,json; print(json.loads(sys.stdin.read())['task_id'])")  echo "Processing task $TASK_ID..."  sleep 5  # Simulate work    # Checkpoint to S3  echo "{\"task_id\": $TASK_ID, \"status\": \"completed\", \"timestamp\": \"$(date -u +%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ)\"}" \    | aws s3 cp - s3://$BUCKET/checkpoint-task-$TASK_ID.json  echo "Checkpoint saved: task $TASK_ID"    # Delete message from queue (acknowledge completion)  aws sqs delete-message \    --queue-url $QUEUE_URL \    --receipt-handle "$RECEIPT" \    --region us-east-1  echo "Task $TASK_ID complete. Message deleted from queue."done

    Figure 35: 📸 Worker processing tasks — checkpoints saved to S3 after each task

  3. Go to your S3 bucket and verify the checkpoint files are appearing as each task completes. You should see checkpoint-task-1.json, checkpoint-task-2.json, etc.

    Figure 36: 📸 S3 bucket — checkpoint files confirm completed tasks are durably saved

Part C — Simulate Interruption and Resume

  1. Send 5 more messages to the SQS queue (task_id 6–10). Start the worker script again on the Spot instance. After it processes task 6 and 7, manually terminate the Spot Instance from the EC2 Console (simulating an AWS interruption).

  2. Launch a new Spot Instance. SSH into it and run the same worker script. Notice it picks up from task 8 — tasks 6 and 7 were already deleted from the queue (acknowledged after completion). The new worker continues without any data loss.

    Figure 37: 📸 New worker resumes from task 8 — tasks 6 and 7 not re-processed

Part D — Cleanup

  1. Terminate the Spot Instance. Go to SQS → select job-queue → Delete. Go to S3 → select the bucket → delete all objects first → then delete the bucket.

✅ Verification: No running instances, no SQS queue, no S3 bucket. You should be able to explain this system in an interview: "I built a Spot-based worker that processes jobs from SQS and checkpoints progress to S3. When an instance is interrupted, the next instance picks up from the SQS queue — only unacknowledged tasks re-run. This gives us 90% cost savings with zero job loss."

⚡ This is the answer to: "How do you handle Spot interruptions in production?" — one of the most common cloud architecture interview questions. You now have a working example, not just a textbook answer.
