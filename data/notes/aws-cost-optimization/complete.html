📌 Introduction

When you're just learning AWS, pricing can feel like an afterthought. But the moment you build something real — a web app, an AI chatbot, a data pipeline — you quickly realize that cost is a first-class concern, not an afterthought. Think about it this way: a single API call to a language model might cost three cents. That sounds like nothing. But if a million customers use your app every day, that's $30,000 a day, just on tokens alone.

This is why Cost Optimization is one of the six pillars of the AWS Well-Architected Framework, alongside Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, and Sustainability. It's not about being cheap — it's about being smart and intentional with your resources so you can do more with what you have.

💡 The Well-Architected Framework has six pillars: Operational Excellence, Security, Reliability, Performance Efficiency, Sustainability, and Cost Optimization. Cost Optimization is the focus of this session.

💰 1. AWS Pricing Key Principles

AWS pricing is built on a few core ideas that are worth internalizing because they shape every decision you'll make about architecture and budget.

Three Fundamental Drivers of Cost

No matter which AWS service you use, your bill ultimately comes from three things:

  • Compute — the processing power (CPU, memory) to run your workloads
  • Storage — the space to hold your data, whether in S3, EBS, RDS, or other stores
  • Outbound data transfer — sending data out from AWS to the internet or between regions
⚡ Notice the asymmetry: AWS typically doesn't charge you for ingesting data in, but does charge when you send it out. This makes sense — outbound means your customers are receiving value from your app, which is why you're running infrastructure in the first place.

Trade Fixed Costs for Variable Costs

Traditional IT meant buying servers upfront — a huge capital expense (CapEx) — whether you used them fully or not. AWS flips this to operational expense (OpEx): You pay only for what you consume, with no upfront commitment required on the default On-Demand model. This is one of the biggest financial arguments for moving to the cloud.

Transparent, Independent Pricing

Every AWS service is priced independently and that pricing is publicly visible at all times. There's no haggling, no salespeople, no hidden sticker shock. You can look up exactly what something costs before you build it. This transparency builds trust and lets you plan your architecture around cost from day one.

Use the Right Pricing Model for the Job

AWS offers four main pricing models: On-Demand, Savings Plans, Spot Instances, and Reserved Instances/Capacity. Each is suited to a different usage pattern. Choosing the wrong model can mean overpaying significantly. We'll cover each in the next section.

✅ Key Takeaway: Cost optimization starts at the architecture design phase — not after your bill arrives. Think about compute, storage, and data transfer patterns before you deploy.

🏷️ 2. AWS Pricing Models

AWS doesn't have one-size-fits-all pricing. Different workloads have different predictability and flexibility, so AWS offers multiple ways to pay. Understanding these models is essential for the AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner exam and for real-world cost management.

Figure 2: AWS Pricing Models Compared

On-Demand Instances

This is the default — pay by the hour (or second, for some services) with no upfront commitment. It's like renting a car day-by-day: flexible, but not the cheapest option if you know you'll need the car all year.

  • Best for: unpredictable or short-term workloads, dev/test environments, or applications you're building for the first time
  • No upfront payment, no long-term commitment
  • Full flexibility to start and stop anytime

Reserved Instances / Savings Plans

If you know you'll need compute for a year or more, you can commit to that usage upfront and AWS rewards you with a significant discount. Reserved Instances can save up to 72% compared to On-Demand pricing, with 1-year or 3-year commitment terms.

  • Best for: steady-state workloads with predictable usage (production databases, always-on web servers)
  • You're essentially telling AWS: "I'll definitely be using this — give me a deal"
  • Savings Plans are a more flexible version that apply across EC2, Fargate, and Lambda
💡 Analogy: Reserved Instances are like buying a yearly gym membership vs. paying for each individual class. If you know you'll go regularly, the annual deal saves you a lot of money.

Spot Instances

AWS has unused EC2 capacity sitting around at any given moment. Spot Instances let you bid on that spare capacity at dramatically reduced prices — sometimes up to 90% off On-Demand rates. The catch? AWS can reclaim Spot Instances with two minutes' notice when that capacity is needed elsewhere.

  • Best for: fault-tolerant, flexible workloads — batch processing, data analysis, rendering, ML training jobs
  • Not suitable for: anything that can't be interrupted (live databases, real-time user-facing apps)
⚡ Think of Spot Instances like borrowing a neighbor's cup of sugar — great when available, but you can't count on it always being there.

Dedicated Hosts vs. Dedicated Instances

Most of the time, AWS runs your workloads on shared physical hardware (multi-tenant). But sometimes — especially for compliance or software licensing reasons — you need dedicated hardware.

  • Dedicated Instance: Your instance runs on hardware dedicated to your account, but AWS can move it between physical servers as needed
  • Dedicated Host: You get an entire physical server reserved specifically for you, with visibility into the hardware. Dedicated Hosts are the most expensive EC2 option, but necessary when software licenses are tied to a specific hardware fingerprint (e.g., certain Oracle or Windows Server licenses)

Regional Pricing Differences

The same AWS service can have different pricing depending on which region you deploy in. Several factors drive these differences — covered in detail in the next section. As a general rule, US East (N. Virginia) tends to be among the cheaper regions, while regions in Asia-Pacific or the Middle East may cost more. Always compare region pricing when designing architecture — especially for large-scale deployments where even small per-unit differences add up significantly.

🌍 4. How AWS Calculates Prices by Region

A question that comes up constantly: why does the same EC2 instance cost more in Mumbai than in US East? It's not random. AWS pricing per region is the result of at least six compounding factors. Understanding them helps you make smarter architecture decisions — not just picking the cheapest region, but picking the right total-cost region for your users.

Figure 8: How AWS Calculates Prices in Different Regions

1. Infrastructure Cost in That Region

AWS builds and operates data centers in each region. The cost of running those data centers varies dramatically by location — and those costs get passed into pricing.

Factors that drive infrastructure cost:

  • Electricity cost — power is much cheaper in some US states than in South Asia or Europe
  • Land and construction — real estate near major cities (Mumbai, Frankfurt) costs more than rural Oregon or Virginia
  • Cooling requirements — hot climates need more cooling infrastructure
  • Local hardware logistics — importing and maintaining server hardware in some regions is more expensive
⚡ Example: US East (N. Virginia) has some of the cheapest infrastructure costs globally — older, fully amortized data centers, cheap electricity, and economies of scale from being AWS's largest region. Mumbai has higher real estate and energy costs, which is directly reflected in its pricing.

2. Supply and Demand

Pricing is also influenced by how heavily a region is used. Popular, mature regions with high utilization can sometimes have higher baseline pricing, while newer regions with lower demand may offer competitive introductory pricing to attract customers. This dynamic is especially visible with Spot Instance prices, which fluctuate in real time based on available spare capacity in each region and Availability Zone.

3. Local Taxes and Regulations

Every AWS region operates under the laws of its country. Compliance costs get baked into the pricing:

  • India: GST (Goods and Services Tax) applies. Compliance with local data residency laws adds overhead.
  • Europe: GDPR compliance requirements add engineering and operational cost to running EU regions
  • Brazil: High import duties and tax complexity make São Paulo one of the most expensive AWS regions
💡 AWS doesn't absorb these costs — they're reflected in the regional price. This is why two technically identical regions in different countries will have different price tags.

4. Network and Data Transfer Cost

This is a big one that beginners often overlook. Data transfer pricing isn't flat — it depends on where data travels:

  • Distance between regions: longer physical distance = more network infrastructure = higher cost
  • Internet exchange costs: connecting to local internet exchanges varies significantly by country
  • Bandwidth pricing locally: internet bandwidth costs differ by country
⚡ Practical example: Data transfer from Mumbai to US East is more expensive than data transfer within the US. Even if your EC2 instance in Mumbai is slightly cheaper, heavy cross-region data transfer can more than offset that savings.

This is also why data transfer OUT from AWS to the internet is always charged, while data transfer IN is generally free — outbound means your app is serving customers, which is the value-generating event.

5. Currency and Purchasing Power

AWS doesn't simply convert USD prices to local currency at market rates. They actively adjust pricing based on:

  • Local market expectations and willingness to pay
  • Purchasing power parity in that country
  • Competitive landscape — what do local and regional cloud providers charge?

The result: two regions with near-identical infrastructure costs can still have different prices because AWS has calibrated pricing to what the local market can support and what makes business sense competitively.

6. Service Availability Maturity

Not all AWS regions offer all services, and not all regions are equally optimized:

  • Older regions (US East, EU West): fully optimized infrastructure, amortized setup costs, economies of scale from years of investment — generally cheaper per unit
  • Newer regions (Middle East, Africa, newer Asia-Pacific): higher setup costs, fewer optimizations, smaller customer base — generally more expensive per unit until they mature

7. Hidden Cost Differences (The Trap Most Beginners Fall Into)

Even if compute prices look similar between two regions, the total cost of running the same architecture can differ significantly due to:

  • NAT Gateway pricing — varies by region
  • Data transfer rules — cross-AZ and cross-region transfer rates differ
  • Load balancer costs — Application Load Balancer hourly rates vary
  • Storage tier pricing — EBS and S3 prices are not identical across regions
❌ The Trap: Comparing only EC2 instance prices across regions and picking the cheapest. The same architecture can cost meaningfully more in one region vs. another once all the ancillary services are factored in.

The Mental Model: AWS Regional Pricing Formula

A simple way to think about it:

AWS Price (per region) =
  Infrastructure cost
  + Supply & demand premium
  + Local taxes & compliance
  + Network & data transfer costs
  + Market strategy adjustment

🗄️ 5. Storage Pricing Deep Dive (S3 & EBS)

Storage pricing has a lot of nuance that beginners often miss. Understanding tiering and volume discounts can lead to dramatic cost savings.

Amazon S3 Pricing

S3 charges are based on the volume of data you store, and AWS uses tiered pricing — the more you store, the lower the per-GB rate. For example, the first 50 TB might cost slightly more per GB than the next 450 TB, which costs less than anything above 500 TB. For very large data users (think ML training datasets), these fractional-cent differences translate into real savings at scale.

Beyond raw storage, S3 offers multiple storage classes that let you trade access speed for cost:

Figure 4: Amazon S3 Storage Classes — Cost vs. Access Speed

  • S3 Standard: Frequently accessed data — highest cost but instant retrieval
  • S3 Intelligent-Tiering: Automatically moves data between tiers based on access patterns — great if you're unsure how often data will be accessed
  • S3 Standard-IA (Infrequent Access): Data you need occasionally but must retrieve quickly — lower storage cost, small retrieval fee
  • S3 One Zone-IA: Same as Standard-IA but stored in only one AZ — cheaper, but less resilient
  • S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval: Archive data with millisecond retrieval
  • S3 Glacier Flexible Retrieval: Archive data retrieved in minutes to hours — significantly cheaper
  • S3 Glacier Deep Archive: Lowest cost storage on AWS — retrieval takes hours. Uses tape drives. Best for data you're legally required to keep but will almost never access (e.g., audit logs, archived healthcare records)
⚡ Real-World Use Case: In the US, HIPAA regulations require healthcare providers to retain patient records for 7+ years. Deep Archive lets organizations meet this requirement at fractions of a penny per GB — far cheaper than keeping cold data in Standard S3.
💡 You can use S3 Lifecycle Policies to automatically move objects between storage classes. For example: "If this object hasn't been accessed in 30 days, move it to Standard-IA. If not accessed in 90 days, move it to Glacier." This automation means you don't have to manually manage data tiering.

Amazon EBS Pricing

EBS (Elastic Block Store) is the persistent disk storage attached to EC2 instances. Pricing varies based on the volume type you choose:

  • gp3 / gp2 (General Purpose SSD): Balanced price/performance for most workloads — databases, boot volumes, dev environments
  • io1 / io2 (Provisioned IOPS SSD): High-performance, low-latency — critical databases that require consistent IOPS. Most expensive option.
  • st1 (Throughput Optimized HDD): Large sequential workloads — log processing, data warehouses. Cheaper than SSD.
  • sc1 (Cold HDD): Lowest cost EBS option — infrequently accessed data that needs a block storage interface

Choosing the right EBS volume type for your actual workload — rather than defaulting to the most performant — is a straightforward way to save money without sacrificing what you actually need.

Free Tier Storage

AWS offers a free tier for storage services that's genuinely useful for learning and small projects. S3 offers 5 GB of Standard storage free for 12 months. EBS offers 30 GB of General Purpose SSD free for 12 months. Always check free tier limits before assuming you'll incur charges.

🧮 6. AWS Pricing Calculator

Before you build anything significant on AWS, you should estimate what it will cost. The AWS Pricing Calculator is a free, publicly available web tool at calculator.aws that lets you do exactly that — no AWS account required.

What Can It Do?

  • Transparent Pricing: See itemized cost breakdowns for any service configuration before you commit
  • Compare regions: Estimate the same architecture in different AWS regions to find the most cost-effective option
  • Compare pricing models: See On-Demand vs Reserved vs Spot costs side by side for the same instance
  • Hierarchical estimates: Group services by project, team, or environment — useful for enterprise planning
  • Export estimates: Share estimates as links or export them for budget presentations

Figure 5: AWS Pricing Calculator Workflow

How to Use It (Practical Example)

Let's say you want to estimate the cost of running an EC2 instance. You'd select your region (e.g., US West Oregon), choose EC2 as your service, then configure the instance type, operating system, tenancy (shared vs. dedicated), and expected utilization. The calculator will immediately show you monthly and annual cost estimates for On-Demand, Reserved 1-year, and Reserved 3-year options.

⚡ From the demo: A dedicated host A1 instance (16 physical cores) priced On-Demand costs around $327/month. The same workload on a nano shared instance costs just a few dollars per month. The difference comes down entirely to instance sizing — which illustrates why right-sizing is so important.
✅ Best Practice: Use the Pricing Calculator when designing architecture. Ask yourself: "What would this cost if I added a Knowledge Base for my AI app? What if I use Aurora instead of RDS MySQL? What if I move from On-Demand to a 1-year Reserved plan once I'm confident in my usage patterns?"

Limitations to Know

The calculator gives you estimates, not guarantees. Actual costs depend on actual usage patterns, data transfer volumes, and API call frequency. Use it for planning and comparison, but monitor your real costs with Cost Explorer once deployed.

✂️ 7. Ways to Reduce Your AWS Bill

Here are the most impactful actions you can take to reduce AWS costs — organized by service category. Many of these directly correspond to findings that AWS Trusted Advisor will surface in your account.

Compute (EC2 & Lambda)

  • Right-size your instances: Don't start with the largest instance available "just in case." Start small, monitor utilization in CloudWatch, and scale up if needed. A t3.micro or t3.small is often enough for learning and dev environments.
  • Stop instances when not in use: Unlike terminating (which deletes the instance), stopping preserves the instance and its data but stops the compute clock. Great for dev instances you only use during business hours.
  • Use Spot Instances for flexible workloads: Batch jobs, ML training runs, and non-time-sensitive processing can often run on Spot Instances at 60-90% savings.
  • Use Compute Savings Plans: Once you have a stable baseline of Lambda and EC2 usage, committing to a Savings Plan locks in discounts of up to 66% versus On-Demand.
❌ Common Mistake: Spinning up large EC2 instances for a lab and forgetting to stop or terminate them. Even "stopped" instances still incur storage charges for attached EBS volumes. Terminate completely if you don't need the data.

Storage (EBS & S3)

  • Snapshot and delete idle EBS volumes: If an EBS volume is attached to a stopped instance you no longer need, take a snapshot (cheap archive) then delete the volume (which is more expensive persistent storage).
  • Use S3 storage tiers: Move infrequently accessed objects to Standard-IA, Glacier, or Deep Archive using Lifecycle Policies. Even a 30-day trigger can dramatically reduce costs for log files and audit data.

Databases (RDS, Redshift, DynamoDB)

  • Stop RDS instances when not needed: Like EC2, RDS instances can be stopped (for up to 7 days at a time per AWS policy) to avoid hourly charges during off-hours.
  • Pause Redshift clusters: Redshift clusters can be paused when not actively querying, stopping compute charges while preserving data.
  • Use DynamoDB Auto Scaling or On-Demand mode: Rather than over-provisioning read/write capacity units, let DynamoDB automatically scale to demand or use On-Demand mode which charges per request.
  • Use Reserved Instances for steady-state databases: Production RDS, Redshift, and ElastiCache instances that run 24/7 are perfect candidates for Reserved Instances.

Networking

  • Delete idle load balancers: Load Balancers charge by the hour regardless of traffic. If a load balancer isn't receiving traffic, delete it.
  • Review AutoScaling Group configurations: Misconfigured ASGs can hold more instances than necessary during low-traffic periods.
  • Consider IPv6 migration: AWS now charges $0.005/hour (~$3.60/month) for each public IPv4 address to encourage migration to IPv6. If your architecture is IPv4-heavy, this adds up.

🏛️ 8. The Cost Optimization Pillar

The Cost Optimization pillar of the Well-Architected Framework is a set of design principles and best practices that help you build and run systems at the lowest possible cost, without compromising performance, reliability, or security. It's organized around three core practices.

1. Choose the Right Pricing Models

Align your purchasing strategy with your workload characteristics. Use On-Demand for unpredictable workloads, Reserved/Savings Plans for stable baselines, and Spot for fault-tolerant batch jobs. Mixing pricing models within a single architecture is a best practice — use Reserved for your steady-state web tier, and Spot for batch processing.

2. Match Capacity with Demand

The old on-premises model forced you to provision for peak demand and waste resources the rest of the time. AWS lets you dynamically scale capacity to match actual demand:

  • Use Auto Scaling to automatically add and remove EC2 instances based on load
  • Start with smaller instance sizes and scale up only when monitoring confirms you need it
  • Use serverless services like Lambda, which automatically scale to zero when idle and charge only per execution

3. Implement Processes to Identify Resource Waste

Even well-architected systems accumulate waste over time — forgotten instances, idle load balancers, orphaned EBS volumes. Establish regular reviews using:

  • AWS Trusted Advisor — proactively flags underutilized resources and cost-saving opportunities
  • AWS Cost Explorer — visualizes spending patterns over time, broken down by service and region
  • CloudWatch — monitors utilization metrics so you can make data-driven right-sizing decisions
✅ Real Example from Session: The instructor discovered $4,000-5,000/month in SageMaker charges for resources spun up in Europe that had been forgotten. Using Cost Explorer and intentional cleanup brought that to near zero. This illustrates why automated monitoring matters — humans forget things.

🛠️ 9. Cost Management Tools

AWS provides three complementary tools for understanding and managing your costs. Understanding when to use each is important for both the exam and real-world practice.

AWS Pricing Calculator

Use before you build. Plan and estimate costs for proposed architectures without needing an AWS account. Accessible at calculator.aws.

AWS Trusted Advisor

Use to audit your existing account. Trusted Advisor continuously analyzes your AWS environment and provides recommendations across five categories: Cost Optimization, Performance, Security, Fault Tolerance, and Service Limits. For cost optimization specifically, it will flag:

  • Underutilized EC2 instances (low CPU usage over an extended period)
  • Idle load balancers
  • Unattached EBS volumes
  • Reserved Instance utilization — whether you're actually using the RIs you've purchased
💡 Access: Basic Trusted Advisor checks (including core cost optimization and security checks) are available to all AWS customers for free. More detailed checks require a Business or Enterprise Support plan. In the AWS Console, search for "Trusted Advisor."

Trusted Advisor is free for all AWS accounts and provides recommendations across Cost Optimization, Security, Performance, Fault Tolerance, and Service Limits.

Figure 7: AWS Trusted Advisor — Five Check Categories

AWS Cost Explorer

Use to analyze past and current spending. Cost Explorer is a free visualization tool built into the AWS Console that lets you view your spending history, break it down by service or region, and identify trends. You can:

  • View monthly or daily cost breakdowns going back 12 months
  • Filter by service (e.g., "How much did I spend on SageMaker last month?")
  • Filter by region (e.g., "Which region is costing me the most?")
  • View Reserved Instance utilization and recommendations
  • Forecast future costs based on current usage trends

AWS Cost Explorer is a free tool that provides visual representations of AWS costs and usage over time, with filtering by service, region, and linked accounts.

✅ Best Practice: Set up a monthly Cost Explorer review habit. Even a 5-minute check can surface forgotten resources, unexpected data transfer charges, or services that are growing faster than anticipated.

📝 Overall Summary

  • Three cost drivers: All AWS costs come down to compute, storage, and outbound data transfer — understanding this helps you predict where your bill will come from.
  • On-Demand is flexible but expensive: The default pay-as-you-go model is great for unpredictable workloads but costs the most per unit of compute.
  • Reserved Instances save up to 72%: Committing to 1- or 3-year terms for stable workloads dramatically reduces costs — the trade-off is flexibility.
  • Spot Instances offer the deepest discounts: Up to 90% off for interruptible, fault-tolerant workloads like batch jobs and ML training.
  • S3 has eight storage classes: Moving infrequently accessed data down through tiers (Standard → IA → Glacier → Deep Archive) can reduce storage costs by orders of magnitude.
  • Right-sizing is the fastest win: Most AWS bills have waste from over-provisioned instances — starting small and scaling based on CloudWatch data is a best practice.
  • Pricing Calculator helps you plan before building: Free, no account required, available at calculator.aws.
  • Trusted Advisor audits your existing account: Free recommendations for cost, security, performance, and fault tolerance.
  • Cost Explorer shows you what you've already spent: Visualize historical spend by service and region to find surprises and trends.
  • Cost Optimization is a Well-Architected pillar: It's not an afterthought — it's one of six foundational pillars alongside Security, Reliability, and Performance Efficiency.

🎯 Objective: Set up automated storage class transitions using Lifecycle Policies

⏱️ Time: 30 min

  6. Add transition: Move to Glacier Flexible Retrieval after 60 days

Project 7: Cost Audit — Find and Fix a Messy Account

🎯 Objective: Simulate a real cloud engineer's first week — audit an account and eliminate waste

⏱️ Time: 90 min

💰 Cost: ~$0.50–2 to create the mess; all recovered when cleaned up

Part A — Create the Mess

  1. Launch a t2.micro EC2 instance — leave it idle (do not SSH in or run anything)
  2. Create an EBS volume — do NOT attach it to any instance
  3. Create an S3 bucket — upload files but set NO lifecycle policy
  4. Create an Application Load Balancer with no targets registered
  5. Leave everything running for at least 2 hours

Part B — Run the Audit

  1. Go to AWS Trusted Advisor → Cost Optimization category

📸 Screenshot 10: Trusted Advisor findings — flagged items appear with estimated monthly savings. Your idle EC2, unattached EBS, and idle load balancer should all appear here

  1. Open Cost Explorer → group by Service → identify each service's cost
  2. Filter by "ElasticLoadBalancing" — confirm the ALB is generating charges with zero traffic

📸 Screenshot 11: Cost Explorer during audit — the Load Balancer charge is visible even with zero traffic, demonstrating that provisioned resources always cost money regardless of usage

Part C — Fix Everything

  1. Stop (or terminate) the idle EC2 instance
  2. Delete the unattached EBS volume
  3. Add a Lifecycle Policy to the S3 bucket (transition to Standard-IA at 30 days)
  4. Delete the idle Application Load Balancer
  5. Take a screenshot of Cost Explorer the next day to confirm charges have stopped
✅ Portfolio note: Document this as "performed cloud cost audit, identified $X/month in waste across EC2, EBS, S3, and ELB, reduced monthly spend by Y%." This is exactly the language cloud engineers use in job applications.

Project 8: Auto Scaling Cost Optimization

🎯 Objective: Configure Auto Scaling so you pay for peak capacity only during peak periods

⏱️ Time: 60 min

💰 Cost: ~$0.50–2 depending on how long you run it

  1. Go to EC2 → Auto Scaling Groups → Create Auto Scaling Group
  2. Create a Launch Template: t3.micro, Amazon Linux 2, default settings
  3. Set capacity: Minimum 1, Desired 1, Maximum 3
  4. Add scaling policy: Target Tracking, CPU utilization target = 50%

📸 Screenshot 12: Auto Scaling Group configuration — the group will automatically add instances when CPU exceeds 50% and remove them when load drops

  1. SSH into the running instance and run: stress --cpu 2 --timeout 120
  2. Watch the Auto Scaling Group Activity tab — new instances should launch within 3–5 minutes
  3. After the stress test ends, watch instances terminate automatically

📸 Screenshot 13: Auto Scaling activity log — scale-out (launch) events triggered by high CPU, followed by scale-in (terminate) events once load dropped. You only paid for the extra instances during the load spike.

⚡ Cost insight: Without Auto Scaling you would provision 3 instances 24/7 to handle peak. With Auto Scaling you run 1 instance normally and scale to 3 only during peaks — reducing your baseline compute cost by up to 66%.

Cleanup: Delete the Auto Scaling Group → this automatically terminates all instances it manages.

Project 9: Serverless vs EC2 Cost Comparison

🎯 Objective: Compare idle cost and active cost between always-on EC2 and pay-per-request Lambda

💰 Cost: Lambda free tier covers 1M requests/month — this project is effectively free

  1. Go to Lambda → Create Function → Author from scratch
  2. Name: "cost-comparison-test", Runtime: Python 3.12, default permissions
  3. Replace the default code with a simple response: return {"statusCode": 200, "body": "Hello"}
  4. Deploy and Test — note the execution time shown in the output (typically 1–3ms)

📸 Screenshot 14: Lambda execution result — note the billed duration in milliseconds. Lambda charges per 1ms of execution, so an idle Lambda function costs exactly $0.00

Project 11: Hybrid Pricing Strategy Design

🎯 Objective: Design a production-realistic mixed pricing model that mirrors how real companies architect for cost

⏱️ Time: 60 min (design and documentation only)

💰 Cost: Free — design exercise

Scenario: You are designing infrastructure for an e-commerce platform that has steady baseline traffic, daily sale event spikes, nightly batch processing jobs, and a public-facing API.

Design each component with the right pricing model:

  • Web servers (baseline 24/7): Reserved Instances — known, stable, runs always
  • Web servers (sale event spikes): On-Demand or Spot with fault tolerance — unpredictable timing
  • Nightly batch processing: Spot Instances — flexible timing, can retry if interrupted, deepest discount
  • Public API endpoints: Lambda — pay per request, scales to zero at night, no idle cost
  • Database: Reserved RDS — always on, predictable, 3-year commitment for maximum savings
  • Static assets: S3 + CloudFront — flat, predictable cost at any scale

📸 Screenshot 16: Hybrid pricing architecture — each component uses the pricing model that matches its traffic pattern. This is how production systems at scale are actually designed.

Use the Pricing Calculator to estimate total monthly cost and document your reasoning for each choice.

⚡ Interview tip: If asked "how would you design a cost-optimised AWS architecture?", describing this hybrid model with reasoning for each component demonstrates senior-level thinking. Most candidates just say "use Reserved Instances."

🤔 Reflection Questions

1. You're building a web app that will have steady traffic 24/7. What pricing model should you consider once you've confirmed the baseline compute needed, and why?

💡 Hint

Think about what "steady" usage means for cost predictability — and what AWS rewards when you can predict your usage in advance.

2. A company needs to run nightly ML training jobs. The jobs take 4-6 hours and can tolerate occasional interruptions with automatic checkpointing. What EC2 pricing model should they use and why?

💡 Hint

Consider which pricing model offers the deepest discount and what the tradeoff is in terms of availability guarantees.

3. Your S3 bucket contains 500 GB of application logs. Logs older than 90 days are only accessed during audits (a few times per year). Logs older than 7 years don't need to be accessed at all but must be retained for compliance. What would an efficient storage tiering strategy look like?

💡 Hint

Think about S3 Lifecycle Policies and which storage classes balance cost vs. retrieval speed requirements for each age bucket.

4. What is the difference between stopping an EC2 instance and terminating it? When would you choose each?

💡 Hint

Think about what happens to the instance data and EBS volumes in each case, and the cost implications of each choice.

5. Why does AWS charge for outbound data transfer but generally not for inbound? What does this reveal about the business model?

💡 Hint

Think about when outbound data transfer occurs and who the data is being sent to. When does the value exchange happen in a typical application?

6. You're architecting for a retail company that has massive traffic spikes during holiday seasons and very low traffic in January and February. How would you design the compute layer to be cost-optimal while handling peak load?

💡 Hint

Think about combining pricing models — reserved for the baseline you always need, and something else for the spike periods.
